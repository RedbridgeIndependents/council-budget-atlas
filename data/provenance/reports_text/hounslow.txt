2. Report Summary 
 
 
1. This report sets out Cabinet’s recommendations to Council in respect of the 
General Fund revenue budget for 2026/27 and the resulting level of Council Tax, 
along with the recommended Housing Revenue Account (HRA) budget for 
2026/27, the capital programme for 2026/27 to 2029/30 , the updated Capital 
Strategy, fees and charges, the Treasury Management Annual Strategy, Annual 
Investment Strategy, Statement on Minimum Revenue Provision and Prudential 
and Treasury Indicators.  
 
2. The General Fund budget recommendations result in a Council Tax 
Requirement of £158.437 million, leading to a Band D Council Tax of £1,675.05, 
an increase of 4.99%. The 2026/27 increase in the Council Tax is in two parts, 
firstly an Adult Social Care precept increase of 2%, and secondly a general 
increase in Council Tax of 2.99%. 
 
3. This report also outlines how the One Hounslow Financial Strategy (‘OHFS’) 
provides a framework  for how the financial resources that the council has 
supports Hounslow’s Ambitious for Hounslow corporate objectives and delivery 
plan. 
 
4. Following the July 2025 update to Cabinet , where a 2026/27 budget gap of 
£54.4m was identified , the Council has identified and proposed to Council a 
savings programme of £26.1m. These savings were taken to Overview and 
Scrutiny Report on 26 January 2026. 
 
5. Additionally, the Ministry of Housing, Commun ities and Local Government 
announced the following changes to Local Government funding: 
 
• Fair Funding Review 2.0 which has delivered an addition al £21.3m over 
the July 2025 OHFS estimates (for the 2026/27 budget) 
• A full  business rates reset  which resets the Council’s Business Rates 
Baseline with a renewed government expectation on how much the 
Council will collect in Rates per annum. 
 
6. Finally, this report outlines the medium-term risks and opportunities with regard 
to the current budget assumptions contained within the OHFS. These primarily 
relate to exceptional factors such as high levels of inflation, fluctuating interest 
rates and increased demand for key services. In doing so, it must be recognised 
that the macro-economic financial environment remains uncertain, and it is 
difficult to make a full, definitive and comprehensive assessment of the ongoing 
financial impact of these issues.

3. Background and links to Corporate Priorities 
 
Fairness, responsibility and ambition – a foreword from the Leader 
 
3.1. A lot has changed over the last decade but one thing that has remained 
consistent is our drive to deliver the best, most reliable public services possible, 
to protect the most vulnerable, and to unleash our borough’s huge potential. 
 
3.2. In very simple terms most of the Council’s funding comes from central 
government and that funding has reduced from £123 million in 2010/11 to a low 
of £58 million by 2020/21. It is only in recent years, and with this year’s Fair 
Funding Review, that Hounslow’s grant funding is beginning to reflect the needs 
profile of its residents. Even with the Fair Funding changes, Hounslow’s per 
capita funding will remain £50 per person below than pre-austerity measures. 
 
3.3. Despite the financial challenges , we have responded by protecting public 
services, supporting the most vulnerable and investing in the future of our 
borough. Measures include innovating and investing in our libraries; a five-year 
partnership providing £1.6 million for the Citizen’s Advice Bureau which has so 
far helped residents access £11 million in support ; and rolling out community 
hubs for local people to get support where and when they need it. 
 
Rising to the Challenge 
 
3.4. We are in a stronger position than most because we have successfully faced 
down three major challenges: austerity, inflation and rising demand. 
 
3.5. Austerity: Local Government was cut more than almost any other part of the 
public sector. As a Council, we became as efficient as possible. Since 2010 the 
Council has delivered more than £100 million of savings and efficiencies; all the 
while continuing to deliver high-quality services.  
 
3.6. Inflation: Every 1% inflation adds an extra £4 million in costs for the Council. 
At the inflation peak of 11%, far outstripping government grant levels ; the 
Council had to absorb the difference. 
 
3.7. Rising demand: Our borough is growing, ageing, and remains far too unequal. 
The recent index of multiple deprivation measures Hounslow as more deprived 
than 83% of local authority areas in England.  More people are turning to the 
Council for support. Adult social care, for example, makes up around half our 
spend, with more than 7,000 requests for support each year, alongside around 
400 children we look after who need support.  
 
3.8. We’ve risen to these challenges by providing the best possible value for money, 
prioritising frontline services, and investing to help reduce demand. 
 
3.9. One example of this is in housing. While London councils spend nearly £6 
million a day on Temporary Accommodation, with one in 21 children growing 
up without a permanent home, Hounslow has some of the lowest Temporary 
Accommodation numbers in London. That is because we have invested in

homelessness prevention. Working with the Lampton Group, we have built 
good quality homes, and we have one of the highest completion rates for social 
and affordable housing in London. 
 
3.10. That has always been our approach - investing in people, investing in services, 
investing in the borough. Taking a prevention and proactive approach, investing 
in public services and supporting the most vulnerable has strengthened our 
financial position, not weakened it. 
 
A turning point 
 
3.11. In December 2025 the Government announced the results of its current Fair 
Funding Review which aims to match the level of local funding to the level of 
local deprivation. 
 
3.12. In previous years the Government’s austerity programme affected Hounslow 
such that the level of need far outstripped the funding, despite the efficiency 
measures introduced by the Council. 
 
3.13. Regardless, ambition and robust management means we have a strong record 
of delivery. Our roads are some of the best in London ; and our schools are 
consistently rated ‘Good’ or ‘Outstanding’. Just last year, our Housing Service 
received the second highest rating from the independent regulator, and the 
work of our Health Outreach Team in tackling health inequalities was nominated 
for an award. By most metrics, Hounslow punches well above its weight. But 
we shouldn’t have had to. This announcement just helps to level the playing 
field. 
 
3.14. Fair Funding is no silver bullet. Our finances are in better shape than most other 
London Boroughs because of a relentless focus on financial sustainability, 
innovation, driving efficiency, prioritising frontline services and protecting our 
reserves for on ly the rainiest of rainy days.  However, we still face high costs 
and rising demand. 
 
3.15. That is why we are proposing a 4.99% increase in the Council’s element of 
Council Tax; that is 2% towards supporting our residents in need of adult social 
care, and another 2.99% for wider council expenditure. This  will mean the 
average household paying an extra £1.53 a week from April. 
 
3.16. We know that the last few years have been tough. But in tough times, people 
need properly functioning public services. If any resident thinks they  might be 
eligible for Council Tax Support or any other kind of help, then help is out there. 
 
Backing Our Ambition 
 
3.17. Responsibility has provided the foundation, but ambition and innovation will 
bring new opportunities to life. As always, our guiding star is creating a fairer, 
more equal borough where support is targeted at those who need it most. This 
budget invests in people, services and in our borough’s bright future.  Here are 
some examples of how we are doing that:

4. Proposal and Rationale 
 
4.1. The Council is legally obliged to set a balanced budget each year, which must 
balance service expenditure against available resources. For effective financial 
management the council should also pull together a financial forward -looking 
strategy and plan and upd ate its projections regularly to ensure that it is well 
placed to understand and address any future challenges. 
 
4.2. Within the budget and medium-term position, provision is made for the Council 
to manage risks but also to direct investment towards its corporate priorities to 
create a more equitable and thriving borough. This investment is directed

towards the priorities  outlined in the Council’s Ambitious for Hounslow’s 
corporate plan.  
 
4.3. ‘Ambitious for Hounslow’ is the Council’s key corporate strategy. It sets out the 
Council’s vision for the borough and, taken together with its Delivery Plan and 
Financial Strategy, explains how the Council will deliver and fund that ambition.  
 
4.4. The report contains Cabinet’s recommendations to Borough Council in respect 
of the council’s budgets and the resulting level of Council Tax  for 2026/27, 
having considered options available through a robust budget setting process  
and the outcomes from the governments provisional local government 
settlement that was published in December.  
 
4.5. Also included is the Council’s capital strategy, outlining the borough investment 
plan between 2026/27 and 2035/36 and how that expenditure will be financed. 
 
4.6. The 2026/27 budget marks a turning point for the authority in which the Fair 
Funding Review 2.0 recognises the increased level of need in the borough that 
the Council has had to support with its own diligently saved resources over the 
last decade. The corrected funding levels give the Council certainty through a 
three-year funding package which sees CSP to 2028/29 increase by 37.2% 
above 2025/26 funding levels and 52.2% above 2024/25 levels. The increases 
in CSP include the government assumption that authorities take the maximum 
Council Tax increase for each year of the three-year settlement period.   
 
4.7. The Council understood the extremely challenging budget gap outlined in the 
One Hounslow Financial Strategy Update July 2025 Cabinet paper. To meet 
that challenge, a refresh ed savings and efficiency identification programme 
was set up involving all senior officers to identify and assure delivery of a wide 
range of savings and income generating proposals. This effort has identified 
£26.1m of savings proposal s for delivery in 2026/27. These proposals were 
presented to Overview and Scrutiny Committee on 26 January 2026. 
 
4.8. The combined outcome of Fair Funding, the 2026/27 savings programme and 
the Council Tax increase means that the Council has been able to set a 
balanced budget for next year, that recognises the level of increased demand 
from our most vulnerable residents and delivers on the Council’s priorities.

5. Financial Context 
 
Macro-Economic Outlook 
 
5.1. At the time of writing the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which is the rate of 
inflation that drives the majority of annual cost rises , sits at 3.4% (December 
2025), . This is 1.4% above the Bank of England’s 2% target rate. At the point 
of setting the 2025/26 budget CPI was 2.8%. 
 
5.2. Inflation continues to follow a bumpy path with the Bank of England forecasting 
inflation to increase temporarily before falling back to its 2% target  around 
2027. But the economy may not evolve as expected  and remains exposed to 
the impact of global shocks. For example, global trade tariffs or developments 
in the Middle East could impact prices in some parts of the economy. 
 
5.3. Since the end of the Covid pandemic, inflation has presented the biggest 
financial challenge to the Council’s finances, with the spike in inflation 
correlating to the Council’s use of reserves to balance the budget.  Costs 
increase by c£4m for every 1% increase above the Bank of England’s 2% 
target.  
 
5.4. The Council’s primary source of borrowing to support its capital programme is 
the Public Works Loans Board (PWLB)  and it has 10-year borrowing rate of 
4.5% on average according to current fore casts. Any fluctuations will impact 
on the Council’s capital programme and HRA business plan. Alternative 
funding options (such as capital receipts or CIL) are being  considered to 
maintain the ambition of the capital programme without putting further pressure 
on the revenue budget from borrowing repayments.

growth forecasts over the medium-term.

5.7. The national economic backdrop remains uncertain. G ross Domestic Product 
(GDP) growth looks set to stay modest through out 2026, with quarterly 
performance sensitive to interest rates, household spending, and business 
investment as indicated by the latest central forecast s from the Office for

Provisional Local Government Finance Settlement (PLGFS) and Fair Funding 
Review 2.0 
 
5.11. On 17 December 2025, MHCLG published its Provisional Local Government 
Finance Settlement (PLGFS) for 2026/27 to 2028/29, marking the first time in 
a decade that a multi -year settlement has been announced , giving local 
authorities much more certainty over their medium-term financial strategies. 
There remains an annual update in the latter two years of this settlement, but 
any changes (should changes be made) will be upwards with this settlement 
representing the minimum level of funding.  
 
5.12. The P LGFS is underpinned by the Government’s Fair Funding Review 2.0  
(FFR2.0) which marks the first time funding within the sector has been 
redistributed since 2013/14. The government has been clear about its policy of 
using FFR2.0 to improve the correlation between funding a nd deprivation. By 
2028, MHCLG’s policy states that the funding system will be fairer and simpler: 
 
• Funding will be aligned with need and deprivation 
• Funding will be based on robust evidence and up to date data; and 
• Local authorities will have greater flexibility and certainty over almost 
£47 billion of their income, over the Settlement period and beyond. 
 
5.13. Added to that, there will be a full business rates baseline reset (for the first time 
since the Business Rates Retention Scheme was introduced in 2013/14) , 
major changes to all the Relative Needs Formulas (RNFs) and the 
simplification of many grants into either Settlement Funding Assessments 
(SFA) or into one of four Consolidated Grants.  
 
 
Core Spending Power (CSP) 
 
5.14. CSP is the Government’s measure of the core components of local 
government funding, including the Fair Funding Assessment, assumed income

from council tax (including the adult social care precept), and a small number 
of individually identifiable grants. 
 
5.15. The building blocks of how CSP is calculated remain the same since the last 
funding review in 2013/14, in that it is built up from grant funding (now updated 
using Fair Funding data points) and a MHCLG assumption of full Council Tax 
uplift (with the decision to uplift being taken at local level), plus some smaller 
ring-fenced and consolidated grants. How Hounslow compares to the London 
average is outlined below: 
 
• CSP will increase by 15.1% across England and 16.2% for London 
boroughs by 2028/29 compared to the baseline year of 2025/26. In 
Hounslow, CSP will increase by 37.2%. 
 
• Over that same period, the non-council tax portion of CSP will increase 
by 7.9% across England and 9.2% for London boroughs. In Hounslow, 
the non-council tax portion of grant will increase by 52.1%. 
 
• The Fair Funding element of the settlement is in response to the 
Government consultation over the summer. The PLGFS sets out how 
the government proposes to implement and transition the changes over 
the three-years to 2028/29.

Business Rates 
 
5.25. Councils under the Business Rates Retention Scheme (BRRS) retain a 
proportion of the business rates collected from local businesses, creating a 
direct link between economic development and local funding. While councils 
still collect all rates, they no long er transfer the full amount to central 
government; instead, a pre-defined “local share” is retained (initially 50%), with 
the remainder going into a “central share” that is redistributed nationally. This 
mechanism incentivises authorities to attract and su pport local enterprise —
higher business growth translates into higher retained income —rather than 
relying purely on central grants.  
 
5.26. The scheme embeds both financial rewards and safeguards. Councils that 
achieve above-forecast growth may owe a “levy” to government, preventing 
excessive local benefit; conversely, a “safety net” mechanism ensures they are 
protected if revenues fall sharpl y—both portions are calculated annually with 
government-published guidance.  
 
5.27. From 1 April 2026, a full reset as part of the Fair Funding Review 2.0 will 
recalibrate the system (the first time there has been a reset since BRRS was 
implemented in 2013/14) . All councils will receive new Business Rates 
Baselines (BRBs) and Baseline Funding Levels (BFLs), reflecting updated 
assessments of local need, resulting in a redistributive realignment of retained 
income. Although most historic growth will be redistribu ted, exemptions apply 
for designated areas and qualifying renewable energy schem es. Transitional 
measures will smooth the shift  – current funding levels will be temporarily 
protected while the levy and safety net are fine ‑tuned before phasing back to 
standard levels. 
 
5.28. Under the reset of the business rates retention system, Hounslow’s provisional 
Baseline Funding Level for 2026/27 is  £19.9m greater than the Council’s 
retained business rates budget for 2025/26. Within this the Council has moved 
from paying back to central Government a ‘tariff’ payment of £9.8m in 2025/26, 
to receiving a provisional ‘top-up’ of central Government funding of £11.1m in 
2026/27. This reflects the redistribution of Government funding under the Fair 
Funding Review 2.0, rather than any underlying change in the Council’s 
business rates base. 
 
5.29. Nevertheless, regardless of the business rates funding distribution, the Council 
still needs to collect the rates due to it as a billing authority for business rates 
purposes. The reset also coincides with the revaluation of rateable values and 
the introduction of new multipliers to replace previous discounts . This  will 
introduce short-term uncertainty for business ratepayers as they adjust to the 
new billing arrangements . It is probable that larger business  ratepayers may 
seek to make protective appeals again st existing rateable values in the final 
months of the current valuation list. These factors introduce further risk to the 
Council around business rates retention, underlining the need for the Executive 
Director of Finance and Resources to maintain an appropriate risk reserve for 
business rates volatility.

undertaken have been: 
 
• Budget Challenge Sessions: a “critical friend” review of how each service 
spends its budget to deliver services. This allowed a targeted approach to 
direct savings towards efficiencies, productivity, income and cost recovery. 
 
• Savings Development : Following the Budget Challenge Sessions , a 
structured officer -led assurance process to test deliverability, risk, 
dependencies, and timing before savings proposals progressed 
 
• Reserves Review: A review of reserve purpose, governance, and time 
horizon to simplify categories and prioritise rebuilding depleted risk 
reserves. 
 
• Pressures and rebasing: Use of 2025/26 budget monitoring and demand 
modelling to identify recurring pressures that require either mitigation or 
evidenced rebasing. 
 
• Continuous Updates: Savings proposals and underlying principles were 
regularly taken to Cabinet Members (individual and collectively) for 
feedback 
 
• Pre-decision scrutiny : An overview of the budget  was presented to 
Overview and Scrutiny in November, with a follow -up in January for the 
Committee to review individual proposals (for General Fund and Housing 
Revenue Account)

Reserves 
 
6.47. The Council holds reserves for a range of reasons. CIPFA states in the 
Financial Management Code that “the aim of the authority’s financial reserves 
is to provide funding for investment in future activities and to act as a safety 
net in case of short-term financial challenges.” For this reason, authorities must 
maintain a reserves strategy. 
 
6.48. The requirement for financial reserves is acknowledged in statute. Sections 32 
and 43 of the Local Government Act 1992 require precepting authorities and 
billing authorities in England and Wales to have regard to the level of reserves 
needed for meeting estimated future expenditure when calculating the budget 
requirement. 
 
6.49. The reserves strategy defines the level and purposes for which the Council 
holds reserves. It consists of three key elements: 
 
• Strategy: what the Council is seeking to achieve through holding 
reserves;  
 
• Financial Planning: linking the level of reserves with plans for their use 
over the period of Medium-Term Financial Strategy (MTFS); and  
 

=== PDF PAGE 31 ===
   
 
   
 
• Operational Framework: how the Council determines the level of 
reserves, manages those reserves and plans for their use in line with 
best practice and statutory requirements.  
 
6.50. Reserves that authorities hold fall into two main categories: 
 
• Usable reserves – those that the Council could utilise to fund capital or 
revenue expenditure. Some of these reserves could be applied 
generally but others will have stipulations attached on their use. 
 
• Unusable reserves – these hold unrealised gains or losses for assets 
not yet disposed of and accounting adjustments, which are required by 
statute. These reserves cannot be used to fund capital or revenue 
expenditure. 
 
6.51. Usable Reserves can be grouped even further: 
 
• Unallocated General Balance – working balances held to ensure long 
term solvency and to mitigate risks e.g. the General Fund balance and 
the Housing Revenue Account balance 
 
• Earmarked Reserves – to fund specific projects or investments, or as a 
means to build up funds for known risks or contingencies, e.g. the 
capital financing reserve or the insurance reserve; 
 
• Ring-fenced Reserves – carried forward balances or grant funding 
which have certain conditions or restrictions attached to them 
preventing their general use by the Council, e.g. schools’ balances or 
homeless prevention grant; 
 
• Capital Reserves – amounts held to finance capital expenditure, e.g.  
receipts from asset disposals and capital grants 
 
6.52. The Unallocated General Balance is reviewed every year  to examine risk 
exposure and has been retained at a minimum level of £18.0m. This has been 
included in Appendix H. 
 
6.53. Reserves should only be used for unforeseen emergencies; mitigation of 
known risks; pump priming for investment; smoothing of spending fluctuations; 
or short -term transitional support. Reserves should not be used to support 
ongoing expenditure commitments or as a substitute for delivering an annually 
balanced budget. The proposed reserve position recommended for approval 
in this report is set out in the table 7 below and based on the 2025/26 Quarter

8. Housing Revenue Account 
 
8.1. The Council is the landlord for 13, 500 council homes, including around 900 
sheltered homes for older people. The Council is also the freeholder for more 
than 3,000 leasehold properties. Together, these represent roughly one in 
seven of the borough's total housing stock and are home to almost one in four 
of the borough's population. 
 
8.2. The Local Government and Housing Act 1989 requires the council to maintain 
a ring -fenced budget for its housing stock related income and expenditure, 
known as the Housing Revenue Account (HRA). 
 
HRA Business Plan 
 
8.3. The Council's strategy for spending and borrowing in relation to maintaining its 
housing stock, operating services for tenants and leaseholders, and the design 
and build of new council homes is set out in the 30-Year HRA Business Plan. 
It is a statutory requirement for this to be updated annually, and the refreshed 
Plan is also included on the  February 2026 Cabinet agenda . It must 
demonstrate that the spending plans needed to meet the Council’s objectives 

=== PDF PAGE 40 ===
   
 
   
 
are affordable and that the  HRA remains  financially viable over the short -, 
medium- and long-term. 
 
8.4. To achieve this, the 2026 update to the HRA Business Plan was prepared with 
a view to delivering three key objectives: 
▪ Build resilience into the annual revenue budget (in the form of a flexible 
contingency) 
▪ Create additional borrowing capacity to support the capital investment 
ambition over the medium term 
▪ Increase the HRA General Reserve to 15% of rent turnover (considered 
best practice across the sector) 
 
8.5. The HRA revenue budget  captures income from dwelling rents and service 
charges, all expenditure required to manage and maintain stock, the cost of 
financing capital investment , and provisions needed to maintain sufficient

8.8. The main risks to the delivery of the 2026/27 HRA revenue budget are:

borrowing 
▪ inflation running higher than the allowances included within the budget 
▪ demand for repairs and/or the unit cost of repairs being higher than

▪ delays to the handover of ready to let new build units which will reduce 
rental income

18.4 The most significant risk to delivery of the council’s target to decarbonise 
council operations to achieve net zero by 2030 is funding. The budget required 
to achieve net zero by 2030 is in excess of £100m and that this will not be 
possible via grant funding alone and therefore it will be essential to develop 
business cases for council investment which meet return on investment 
criteria. Additionally, grants are increasingly requiring a higher proportion of 
match-funding which needs to be affordable and meet investment rules for the 
beneficiary (which is not always the council).  
 
18.5 The report  agreed by Cabinet in January 2025  therefore outlined the key 
action that over the coming year a Net Zero Investment Plan will be developed 
to ensure that the pathway to net zero is credible and there can be confidence 
in delivery. This will assess and include a range of funding sources and will be 
overseen by a  new officer Climate Investment Board which is part of the 
governance for the council’s net zero delivery.  
 
18.6 The Net Zero Investment Plan will form the Climate Budget from 2026/27 and 
into future years.
