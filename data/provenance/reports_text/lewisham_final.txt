Outline and Recommendations 
The purpose of this report is to set out the overall financial position of the Council 
in relation to 2025/26 and to set the Budget for 2026/27. This report 
recommends the Council Tax to be agreed and housing rents to be set for 
2026/27. It sets the Capital Programme for the next four years and the Council's 
Treasury Management Strategy for 2026/27. 
The report provides information on the revenue budget reduction proposals 
necessary to unwind the 2025/26 use of Reserves and set the Budget for 
2026/27, the allocation of budget growth to meet inflation pressures and service 
overspending plus the steps to address the budget requirement for 2026/27 and 
future years as part of the Medium-Term Financial Strategy. 
Council is asked to consider the recommendations listed in this report at Section 
2. 
Timeline of Engagement and Decision-Making 
Mayor and Cabinet approved the draft 2026/27 Budget Report on 11 February 
2026. 
Public Accounts Select Committee considered the draft 2026/27 Budget Report 
on the 29 January 2026. 
The Council Tax Base was approved by Council on the 28 January 2026.

1. Summary 
1.1. This report sets out the context and range of budget assumptions which 
Council is required to agree to enable it to set a balanced budget for 2026/27. 
These include the following: 
1.2. General Fund 
 The latest final Local Government Finance Settlement, a new multi-
year Settlement, used to fund the General Fund Revenue Budget, is 
based on Government’s Fair Funding Review and covers 2026/27 – 
2028/29; the Spending Review Period. The new multi-year Settlement 
is built on a Core Spending Power basis and includes these funding 
components: 
o Council Tax Requirement,  
o Fair Funding Allocation,  
o Recovery Grant, 
o Recovery Grant Guarantee,  
o Recovery Grant Uplift, 
o Adjustment Support Grant, and 
o Consolidated Grants (only those within Core Spending Power). 
 In respect of the General Fund, the assumed Core Spending Power, 
and therefore the General Fund Revenue Budget for 2026/27, is 
£425.799m. This is funded by the Fair Funding Allocation, forecast 
Council Tax receipts including an increase in Council Tax of 4.99%, 
Recovery Grant, Recovery Grant Guarantee, Recovery Grant Uplift, 
Adjustment Support Grant and consolidated grants within Core 
Spending Power. 
 The Fair Funding Allocation includes the Baseline Funding Level in 
relation to business rates, the Revenue Support Grant and the Local 
Authority Better Care Grant. Full detail of the 2026/27 General Fund 
Revenue Budget is set out in Section 6. 
 To align Lewisham Council’s 2026/27 General Fund Revenue Budget 
with Government’s Fair Funding Review, it has been built based on a 
restated 2025/26 Core Spending Power Budget of £401.877m as 
opposed to the General Fund Net Revenue Budget of £312.515m. Full 
detail of the restatement and build can also be found in Section 6. 
 The use of £21.895m of Reserves in 2025/26 has been unwound, with 
the detail set out in Section 6. 
 The changes to the prior year General Fund position to meet the 
2026/27 General Fund Budget requirement of £425.799m are 
proposed on the basis of the following assumptions:

 On 28 January 2026, Government confirmed that Registered Providers 
will be able to increase weekly rents for Social Rented homes, currently 
below the formula level, by up to an additional £1 per week over and 
above CPI+1% from 1 April 2027 (ie. rent convergence). This followed 
on from their consultation on how to implement social housing rent 
convergence to bring actual rent levels up to formula rent levels. As it 
was confirmed that rent convergence would not be permitted until 1 
April 2027, rent increases for 2026/27 equate to CPI+1% only. They 
further confirmed that up to an additional £2 per week could be applied 
over and above CPI+1% from 1 April 2028, until formula rent levels are 
reached. 
 Based on this Government decision, there will be no extra rental 
income for the HRA in 2026/27. The HRA Business Plan has been 
updated, and forecast rent levels adjusted, to reflect the additional 
allowance from 2027/28. 
 The proposed Capital Programme (HRA) budget for 2026/27 to 
2030/31 of £602.29m, of which £148.71m is for 2026/27. This is spend 
on both existing stock and for the building of new homes as set out in 
Section 9.   
 Continuing the action plan to manage the problems related to damp 
and disrepair. These include undertaking surveys to fully identify damp 
and disrepair, decency, safety and lifecycle repairs needed in the 
Councils stock. 
 Upon completion of surveys, repairs and follow-up actions are being 
taken to resolve the issues identified. This may require the allocation of 
additional resources which may not be fully forecasted within the 
current HRA budget allocations. Discussions are on-going with service 
heads on how to fund the cost of these repairs which may result in 
capital plans being reprioritised for existing stock.  
1.6. General Fund Income and Grants 
 Section 6 of the report sets out the detail of changes to other sources 
of income. As part of the Fair Funding Review, Government rolled up 
many existing grants into the final Local Government Finance 
Settlement. The Settlement now includes grants both within and 
outside Core Spending Power. 
 The Local Authority Better Care Grant (£18.433m) remains unchanged 
from 2025/26, although it now forms part of the Fair Funding Allocation. 
The Recovery Grant (£5.279m) also remains unchanged from 2025/26. 
Both are within Core Spending Power. 
 Government have introduced the Recovery Grant Guarantee and 
Recovery Grant Uplift from 2026/27. For 2026/27, these are £10.578m 
and £2.991m respectively. In addition, there is the inclusion of the 
Adjustment Support Grant for 2026/27 only, the allocation being 
£0.173m.

Local Government 
5.10. Global and national economic conditions continue to exert significant pressure

major shift in devolution of powers and governance. Although the impact for 
England is significant, London, and therefore Lewisham Council, is currently 
unaffected structurally, with London boroughs remaining unitary in function 
and the GLA remaining as the strategic mayoral authority. 
5.12. London, and Lewisham Council, are however, heavily impacted by the other

Council directly.

Recharge.

The Reformed Local Government Finance Settlement 
6.65. A reform of Government’s approach to funding allocations through the Local 
Government Finance Settlement has been highly anticipated for several 
years, and in the Autumn Budget 2024, it was confirmed that Government 
would deliver a new Settlement for local government following the Spending 
Review 2025. 
6.66. Following the Spending Review 2025, in which Government announced an 
additional £3.3bn of grant funding in real terms for local authorities by 2028/29 
compared with 2023/24, Government consulted with local authorities 
regarding the Fair Funding Review. Within this consultation, they confirmed 
that they would be distributing funds based on local need, cost variations, and

including Children & Young People Services, Adult Social Care and

6.67. The consultation closed on 15 August 2025, after which further

final allocations on 9 February 2026.  
6.68. The objective of this multi-year Settlement is to phase in the new Fair Funding 
Shares for each local authority over the course of the next three years,

separate grants, previously allocated through fragmented funding streams, to 
give each Council an illustrative Core Spending Power that is now more 
comprehensive, bringing together centrally distributed grant funding, 
alongside Council Tax and retained business rates. 
6.69. Government have advised that between 2025/26 and 2028/29, under the 
reformed Settlement, local authorities in England will see an average overall 
real terms increase in Core Spending Power of 2.6% per year. It is vital to 
remember, however, that this includes a presumed 4.99% increase in Band D 
Council Tax every year until 2028/29. In nominal cash terms, Lewisham 
Council will receive increases in Core Spending Power between 2025/26 and 
2028/29 of 5.95%, 0.80% and 1.21% per year respectively, which, for the final 
two years, falls below the expected levels of inflation.

Council Tax Income 
7.9. In considering savings proposals and the level of Council Tax, members make 
political judgements balancing these with their specific legal responsibilities to 
set a balanced budget and their general responsibilities to stewardship of the 
Council’s finances over the medium term.   
7.10. As well as decisions about the level of Council Tax, Council Tax income is 
also affected by growth in the number of properties in the borough, the rate of 
Council Tax collection, and the Local Council Tax Reduction Scheme (CTRS). 
7.11. The government annually sets the limit by which Council Tax can be 
increased locally without triggering the requirement to hold a referendum. The 
level is set at 2% and was applied for 2020/21, 2021/22 and 2022/23. Due to 
exceptionally high levels of inflation, in the Autumn Budget 2022, Government 
lifted the limit to 2.99% for 2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26. The final Local 
Government Finance Settlement on 9 February 2026 confirmed the 
continuation of the 2.99% uplift limit for each year of the Spending Review 
period, as announced in the Spending Review 2025 on 30 June 2025. This 
has been extended to meet the recognised funding pressures faced by the 
sector. 
7.12. The Adult Social Care Precept is in addition to this. It was introduced by 
Government from 2016/17, and the percentages available to levy annually 
have varied over the ten-year period. In 2021/22 government gave local 
authorities the option of levying a 3% Adult Social Care Precept over two 
years. This option was agreed by the Council and included in the 2021/22 
Budget. A further 1% precept was included in the 2022/23 Budget. In light of 
the high cost of care driven by general inflation, local authorities have been 
permitted to levy a 2% Social Care Precept in 2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26. 
To date, Lewisham Council has applied a 20% increase over the ten-year 
period. Government is again allowing councils to apply a precept of 2%, this 
time for each year of the Spending Review period. 
7.13. For 2026/27, Council Tax has been raised by 4.99% in total (pending Mayor 
and Cabinet and full Council approval); the 2.99% core Council Tax increase 
and the 2% Adult Social Care precept as set out above. This has increased 
the Band D amount by £82.07, from £1,644.75 to £1,726.82, and is expected 
to result in additional funding of £9.624m when combined with a 1.27% 
increase in Council Tax Base, and a consistent collection rate of 96%.

Risks 
7.39. Setting a balanced budget is a statutory requirement for a local authority, and 
the Council’s inability to do this without the planned use of Reserves 
introduces additional risk to the long-term financial sustainability of the 
Council. The Council has a good level of Reserves, and, when benchmarked, 
performs well against both London and the UK as a whole. The governments 
data explorer tool shows that, based on 2023/24 data, the Council’s non-
ringfenced Reserves as a percentage of its net revenue expenditure is 95.6%, 
compared to the median of similar local authorities at 50.4% and the rest of 
England at 39.9%. Based on the 2024/25 revenue outturn reports, Lewisham 
had the 5th highest level of total Reserves in London, including DSG and 
schools reserves, and the unallocated financial reserves. However, it’s 
important to note that seven London Boroughs were in receipt of Exceptional 
Financial Support in 2025/26, with more expected in future years. 
7.40. The clarity of the three-year Spending Review means that the Council has 
confirmation that its real terms funding levels will fall over this period, even 
with above inflation increases in Council Tax. The use of Reserves to fund 
previous overspends, set previous budgets and support once-off pressures 
has meant that some of this resilience has been eroded, even if not as quickly 
as other boroughs.   
7.41. It is therefore imperative that the MTFS savings targets are both identified 
early and delivered in full. 
7.42. The second key risk is that of the future funding levels for local authorities 
post the current Spending Review period. Whilst there is clarity with regards 
to 2026/27 – 2028/29, there is no confidence that funding levels will not 
decrease significantly thereafter. Whilst the transitional protections offered are 
all very welcome, such as the late confirmation of the continuing Recovery 
Grant, plus the introduction of the Recovery Grant Guarantee and Uplift, these 
offer potential funding ‘cliff edges’. The combined allocation of these in 
2028/29 is £17.433m, and it remains very likely that these will fall away in 
2029/30. The current MTFS does not extend beyond 2028/29; therefore, the 
summer update, which will include 2029/30, is expected to result in an upward 
revision of the savings target. 
7.43. As part of the reform to local government finance, the Business Rates 
Retention Scheme was reset and to smooth the risks associated with this, a

10. Treasury Management Strategy 2026/27 
10.1. The Council is required to operate a balanced budget, which broadly means 
that cash raised during the year will meet cash expenditure. Part of the 
treasury management operation is to ensure that this cash flow is adequately 
planned, with cash being available when it is needed. Surplus funds are 
invested in low-risk counterparties or instruments commensurate with the 
Council’s low-risk appetite, providing adequate liquidity initially before 
considering investment return. 
10.2. The second main function of the treasury management service is the funding 
of the Council’s capital plans. These capital plans provide a guide to the 
borrowing need of the Council, essentially the longer-term cash flow planning, 
to ensure that the Council can meet its capital spending obligations. This 
management of longer-term cash may involve arranging long or short-term 
loans or using longer-term cash flow surpluses. On occasion, when it is 
prudent and economic, any debt previously drawn may be restructured to 
meet Council risk or cost objectives. 
10.3. The contribution the treasury management function makes to the authority is 
critical, as the balance of debt and investment operations ensure liquidity and 
the ability to meet spending commitments as they fall due, either for day-to-
day revenue purposes or for larger capital projects. Treasury operations will 
see a balance of the interest costs of debt and the investment income arising 
from cash deposits which affects the available budget. Since cash balances 
generally result from reserves and balances, it is paramount to ensure 
adequate security of the sums invested, as a loss of principal will in effect 
result in a loss to the General Fund balance. 
10.4. Whilst any commercial initiatives or loans to third parties will impact on the 
treasury function, these activities are generally classed as non-treasury 
activities, arising usually from capital expenditure, and are separate from the 
day-to-day treasury management activities.

Borrowing Structure and Pooling Approach 
10.56. The Council operates a two-pool borrowing structure, maintaining separate 
notional debt pools for the General Fund (GF) and the Housing Revenue 
Account (HRA). This approach ensures a transparent and equitable 
apportionment of borrowing costs between the two statutory funds and 
reflects sector endorsed good practice for managing HRA and General Fund 
debt independently. 
10.57. A notional exercise is undertaken to allocate existing and future borrowing 
between the General Fund Pool and the HRA Pool, based on each fund’s 
Capital Financing Requirement (CFR) and historic debt attribution. 
10.58. Each pool carries its own share of debt, including its respective proportion of 
both historical and new borrowing. 
10.59. Interest costs are charged separately to the General Fund and HRA, based 
on the characteristics of the loans within each pool, ensuring that neither fund 
subsidises the other. 
10.60. This approach supports fairness, stability, and transparency in the allocation 
of borrowing costs. It also shields the HRA from changes in General Fund 
borrowing activity (and vice versa), helping to maintain a consistent 
Consolidated Rate of Interest (CRI) within each pool. This aligns with CIPFA 
endorsed principles for self-financing authorities and supports robust financial 
planning and compliance with prudential requirements.  
10.61. Managing Borrowing Within Each Pool: New borrowing is allocated to the 
relevant pool based on the purpose of the capital expenditure it finances. 
Internal borrowing, where used, is also allocated separately between the two 
pools, ensuring clarity on under borrowing or over borrowing. Maturity profiles, 
refinancing decisions, and interest rate risk management are monitored and 
reported for each pool in line with the Prudential Code and the Council’s wider 
treasury risk management framework.

Minimum Revenue Provision (MRP) Policy Statement 
10.66. The Council is required to pay off an element of the accumulated General 
Fund capital spend each year (the Capital Finance Requirement - CFR) 
through a revenue charge (the MRP), although it is also allowed to undertake 
additional voluntary payments if required (Voluntary Revenue Provision – 
VRP). The MRP must be determined by the Council as being a prudent 
provision having regard to Capital Finance and Accounting Regulations 2003. 
10.67. The MRP is the amount the Council charges to the revenue account and does 
not correspond to the actual amount of debt repaid, which is determined by 
treasury related issues. MRP is not charged on capital expenditure financed 
by borrowing until the year following the expenditure or the in the financial 
year following the one which the assets become available for use. 
10.68. Historically the Council has applied a consistent MRP policy which comprises 
of previous borrowing being repaid at the rate of 4% (equivalent to 25 years) 
of the outstanding balance.

10.79. When such lease contracts and the related assets and liabilities are brought 
onto the balance sheet, a local authority will increase its long-term liabilities 
and as a result this will increase the debt liability. 
10.80. Regarding MRP in respect of assets acquired either under leases where a 
right-of-use asset is on the balance sheet or where on-balance sheet PFI 
contracts are in place, the prudent charge to revenue can be measured as 
being equal to the element of the rent/charge that goes to write down the 
balance sheet liability.  
10.81. Where a lease (or part of a lease) or PFI contract is brought onto the balance 
sheet, having previously been accounted for off-balance sheet, the MRP 
requirement is regarded as having been met by the inclusion in the charge for 
the year in which the restatement occurs, of an amount equal to the write-
down for that year plus retrospective writing down of the balance sheet liability 
that arises from the restatement. 
Capital Loans 
10.82. Regulation 27(4) allows a local authority to exclude capital loans that are 
financed by debt from the requirement to make MRP, provided the loan is not 
a commercial loan. A commercial loan is defined in regulation 27(5) as a loan 
from the authority to another entity for a purpose which, if the authority were 
to undertake itself, would be primarily for financial return; or, where the loan is 
itself, capital expenditure undertaken primarily for financial return. Local 
authorities must make MRP with respect to any debt used to finance a 
commercial capital loan. 
10.83. A local authority may choose not to charge MRP in respect of the financing by 
debt of a loan issued by an authority to any person or body, where — 
(a) the loan is treated as capital expenditure in accordance with regulation 
25(1)(b), 
(b) the loan is not a commercial loan, and 
(c) the local authority has not recognised, in accordance with proper 
practices(c), any expected or actual credit loss in respect of that loan.

11.28. Explanations of the different funding streams are detailed below: 
 Capital Receipts – These are receipts from the sale of Council assets, 
which are pooled and then used to fund new Capital Projects.  
 Capital and Corporate Reserves – This is money put aside by the 
council either for a specific capital project (for example for Refreshing 
the IT), or as a general reserve for capital projects. 
 Community Infrastructure Levy (CIL) - This is a levy that local 
authorities can choose to charge on new development in their area, 
which local authorities must spend on infrastructure needed to support 
the development of their area. 
 Grants – These are sums of money given to the council, usually from 
central government bodies, which are predominantly for a specific 
purpose detailed in the grant agreement. 
 Prudential Borrowing – This is money borrowed externally which is 
used to finance capital expenditure. The borrowing is then repaid over 

=== PDF PAGE 125 ===

Please give us feedback so we can improve. 
Go to https://lewisham.gov.uk/contact-us/send-us-feedback-on-our-reports   
the useful life of the asset via the Minimum Revenue Provision (MRP). 
This financing option is usually the last resort for when there are no 
other financing options available. 
 Revenue Contribution – Similarly to Capital and Corporate Reserves, 
this is money set aside from revenue fund capital expenditure. 
However, this is set aside from the in-year revenue budget.  
 Right to Buy (RTB) Receipts – These are receipts from housing sales 
that are part of the RTB scheme. These can be used by local 
authorities to fund the development of affordable housing, both new 
build and refurbishment of existing stock, on a one-to-one basis. 
 Section 106 (S106) – Similarly to CIL, these funds are receipts from 
charges made to developers. These are used to mitigate the impacts 
that developments will have on the local community and infrastructure. 
 Major Repairs Reserve – This is a self-financing reserve within the 
HRA, that accounts for the depreciation charge of the Council’s 
housing stock and applies this to finance further HRA capital 
expenditure.

inflation or additional costs such as storage, as well as a potential 
increase in interest costs for projects that require external borrowing to 
fund them. Contingencies can be built into the project cost plans to 
allow for these delays and potential additional costs.  
 The Council may need to make additional savings meaning certain 
schemes may need to be paused/aborted. This risk is especially 
prevalent as the Council is currently using reserves to set a balanced

 Competing priorities for limited resources requires statutory 
requirements (for example Health and Safety works or changes 
resulting from new building regulations) to be met first, potentially 
limiting the number or scale of schemes within the programme. 
 A project may not deliver the expected benefits. This can be mitigated 
by being prudent with any assumptions made when initiating the 
project, as well as building in a defects period to projects to rectify 
scenarios where the benefits are not being delivered due to problems 
with work done by the contractors. 
 A project may not meet the criteria laid out in its funding requirements 
(for example grant funding with date conditions attached expiring due 
to delays to the project), leading to a potential re-payment of this 
funding. If this materialises then there is a possibility some or all of the 
funding would need to be repaid. This can be mitigated by close 
monitoring of the conditions of funding and, in cases where there are 
changes to projects, communication with the funding issuer.

Budget Setting 
15.1. Budget setting is a matter for Council, having considered recommendations

15.2. The Council is obliged by section 151 of the Local Government Act 1972 to 
make proper arrangements for the management of its financial affairs. It must 
set a balanced budget for 2026/27 by 11 March 2026.  
15.3. In approving the recommendations in this report, members must have regard 
to the Local Government Finance Act 1992 which requires the Council to 
ensure that budget estimates are robust and reserves adequate. Under 
section 25 of the Local Government Act 2003, the s151 Officer must provide a 
statement on the robustness of the budget and the adequacy of reserves.  
15.4. The Local Government Act 2003 introduced a prudential system of financial 
control whereby the Council is free to borrow or invest so long as its capital 
spending plans are affordable, prudent, and sustainable. The Council must 
determine and keep under review how much it can afford to borrow having 
regard to CIPFA’s Prudential Code of Capital Finance in Local Authorities. 
The Code requires that in making borrowing and investment decisions, the 
Council must consider affordability, prudence, and sustainability, value for 
money, stewardship of assets, service objectives, and practicality. Members 
are also reminded of their fiduciary duty to the Council Taxpayer, effectively to 
act as trustee of the Council’s resources and to ensure proper custodianship 
of Council funds. Furthermore, section 3 of the Local Government Act 1999 
places the Council under a duty to secure continuous improvement in the way 
its functions are exercised, having regard to a combination of economy, 
efficiency, and effectiveness. Members must have regard to this duty in 
making decisions in relation to this report. 
15.5. The planned use of reserves is lawful, provided members are satisfied that 
this does not compromise the Council’s longer term.
