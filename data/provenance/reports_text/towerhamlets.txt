2. SECTION 25, LOCAL GOVERNMENT ACT 2003 
 
Introduction 
 
2.1 Section 25(2) of the 2003 Act requires Full Council to have regard to the CFO’s 
report when agreeing its annual budget and precept. In addition, Council must 
have regard to this report when making its decisions around the annual budget 
and the level of Council Tax for 2026-27 in accordance with section 31A of the 
Local Government Finance Act 1992. 
 
2.2 Section 26 of the Local Government Act 2003 places an onus on the CFO to 
ensure the Council has established a minimum level of reserves to be retained 
to cover any unforeseen demands that could not be reasonably defined within 
the final the proposed budget.  
 
2.3 Local authorities owe a fiduciary duty to Council taxpayers, which means they 
must consider the prudent use of resources in the short and long term, 
including control of expenditure, the need to strike a fair balance between the 
interests of Council taxpayers and the community’s interest in adequate and 
efficient services, and the need to act in good faith in relation to compliance 
with statutory duties and exercising statutory powers. 
 
2.4 In the London Borough of Tower Hamlets, the CFO (Section 151 Officer), as 
defined in the Local Government Act 1972 is the Acting Corporate Director of 
Resources, a role held by the current CFO on an acting basis since June 2025. 
The S25 and S151 duties are the personal responsibility of the CFO and this 
statement is provided in the exercise of those statutory duties. It represents 
the professional opinion of the CFO based on their time in the role and the 
evidence available to them.  
 
2.5 There is no prescribed format to a section 25 statement, but it is required to be 
presented as part of the Council’s General Fund Revenue Budget, MTFS, 
including Treasury Management Strategy and Housing Revenue Account.

a) £18.9m net General Fund (GF) overspend after applying planned 
substitution of funding from NCIL, CIL, S106 and Public Health of £19.1m 
(subject to ongoing and consistent application of relevant governance 
processes) and net drawdowns of departmental reserves of £18.8m (Q1 
was £14.7m overspend). The overspend has increased since Q1 mainly

c) Prioritising resources to the Corporate Plan and ensuring investment aligns 
with the Council’s agreed priorities. Budget proposals are developed within 
the framework of the Council’s Strategic Plan, with associated delivery risks 
identified, assessed, and where appropriate, mitigated.

Robustness of Budget Estimates  
 
2.16 The London Borough of Tower Hamlets enters the 2026–29 Medium-Term 
Financial Strategy (MTFS) period against a backdrop of significant challenges 
for local government with unprecedented demands on all services, cost of 
living crisis and continual inflationary cost pressures.  
 
2.17 Tower Hamlets is not exempt from these demands. Given local authorities 
statutory duty to provide a range of services where demand is growing, for 
example adult social care, homelessness, and SEND pressure on the 
Council’s finances and services will continue which brings risks through their 
volatility in volumes and costs.  
 
2.18 The Council provides many services to its residents to a high quality and has 
done so for many years with a lower Council tax than its neighbouring 
authorities. This Statement is not about the quality of these services, it is about 
the financial challenges the Council faces, in common with most other councils, 
to continue to provide services on a financially sustainable basis. 
 
2.19 The local government settlement marks the first time in this decade that the 
Government has illustrated multiple years’ worth of funding allocations, 
covering 2026-27 to 2028-29. However, it should be noted that the settlement 
remains an annual event and figures for future years are subject to change 
until they are ‘locked in’ by their own settlement. It is important to place this 
budget and the 3-year MTFS a in the context of a highly uncertain funding 
environment for local government. The Fair Funding Review 2.0 has significant 
implications for the council’s future government funding with a potential risk 
arising in 2029-30. In a worst-case scenario, in the next Spending Review, 
funding may fall in real terms. 
 
2.20 Members need to be mindful of other material risks. There is a real possibility 
that inflation and pay awards may outstrip the forecasts assumed within the 
MTFS, placing further pressure on the Council’s budget. Demand for services, 
particularly in high‑risk and volatile areas, continues to exceed already 
elevated trends, placing sustained upward pressure on the Council’s financial 
position.  The delivery of both future savings proposals and transformation 
programme may face operational and delivery challenges. Income budgets 
can be subject to changes in both demand and economic conditions and can 
be volatile. 
 
2.21 Whilst the Acting Director of Resources (s151 officer) ensures that the overall 
MTFS process drives forward, it is a collaborative effort with the Mayor, 
Cabinet Member for Finance, other Cabinet Members, Chief Executive, 
Corporate Directors, and Heads of Service. In addition, Overview & Scrutiny 
Committee provides pre decision review of the proposals. 
 
2.22 The MTFS is a continuous process and the journey ending with the production 
of this report has been over ten months in the making. Estimates are made at

the budget is set on the best available information as at budget setting. 
 
2.23 The MTFS development process begins with calculating the estimated budget 
gap, based on assumptions for pay and price inflation, demand and 
demographic pressures, interest rates, income forecasts (including Council 
Tax and Business Rates collection rates), Pension Fund contribution rates, 
capital expenditure and receipts, bad debt provisions, external grants, and 
required growth and savings.

chaired by the Mayor and supported by the Cabinet Member for Finance to 
develop in year mitigation plans for 2025-26 and 2026-27 MTFS Budget 
reduction proposals. All savings and growth proposals have been subject to a 
structured review process, including multiple FSF and SRP meetings, where 
Lead Members and Corporate Directors were collaboratively challenged and

departmental spending reviews through their Directorate Leadership Team 
meetings, where they consider savings and growth proposals before being 
submitted to the Spending Review panels. These sessions provide structured 
oversight of financial performance, ensuring that mitigations, in‑year 
management actions, and financial forecasts are fully discussed, challenged, 
and agreed as part of the Council’s wider financial governance framework. 
 
2.26 Proposals for growth, savings and income generation are ultimately a matter

the available resources. In balancing such decisions Members must have 
regard to the professional advice of officers in such matters as service levels, 
statutory responsibility, demographic factors (particularly in respect of 
demand-led services), and unavoidable cost pressures whilst always having 
regard to the need to remain within the statutory requirement to balance the 
budget and to keep within that budget and available resources once the budget 
is set. This report forms part of that advice.

Housing Revenue Account (HRA) 
2.33 The HRA Business Plan is based on assumptions, these include assumptions 
on rent levels, rent convergence, inflation, Right to Buy sales, interest rates on 
borrowing, capital expenditure grant income and savings. Independent advice 
was sought from the Council’s advisors, who were able to use their sector 
knowledge to provide independent checks that the assumptions feeding into 
the HRA business plan are realistic and challenge where they were 
significantly different to what was being modelled in other Councils or went 
against their professional judgement.  The final decision, however, remains 
with the Council.   
 
2.34 The HRA Business Plan is in a balanced position across all 30 years with no 
breach of its key operating parameters, that being an Interest Cover Ratio of 
1.15 and minimum revenue balances of £10m.  However, there is very little 
headroom in the early years of the plan and any changes to delivery adversely 
impacting income or expenditure is likely to result in a breach of the interest 
cover ratio. 
 
2.35 Risks associated with the HRA business plan which need to be managed to 
ensure its viability include:  
 

=== PDF PAGE 12 ===
a) Timing of acquisitions and new build properties.  Any slippage to these 
delivery programmes will result in a reduction in rental income.  This income 
loss is compounded over the life of the business plan.  As income from rents 
represents the main funding source of the HRA, it has a significant impact 
on the HRA’s ability to meet the cost of interest payments on its borrowing.  
 
b) Delays to delivery of the capital programme also leads to additional costs.  
Labour and material costs are rising at above inflation levels and later 
delivery to both new build and existing stock rolling programmes will put the 
viability of the business plan at risk.  Delays to major works on the existing 
stock programme will also have a revenue impact, with more reactive 
repairs required to maintain the ageing stock. 
 
c) As part of the national Social and Affordable Housing Programme (SAHP), 
the Council’s new‑build programme assumes future GLA grant funding of 
£52m in 2026-27 and £156m across the life of the capital programme. This 
funding is not yet approved, and failure to secure the full allocation would 
risk breaching the Interest Cover Ratio. Schemes reliant on external grants 
should only proceed once funding is confirmed and all governance gateway 
requirements have been met. Progressing schemes without confirmed 
grant funding would create a funding gap requiring reductions to the capital 
programme, additional borrowing (subject to prudential borrowing 
requirements), or further savings, and any abortive costs would need to be 
met by the HRA.  
 
d) The cost of delivering the capital programme, and the resulting viability of 
individual projects, presents a significant risk to the business plan. Unit 
costs have risen sharply and continue to face inflationary pressures, 
increasing the likelihood of schemes exceeding budget. Where projects 
cannot be delivered within approved cost envelopes, they will need to be 
scaled back, alternative savings identified, or additional borrowing 
considered, subject to Prudential Borrowing requirements. Any departures 
from approved budgets will also necessitate reassessment of programme 
scope, and any abortive costs incurred would fall to the HRA. 
 
e) Over the past decade the Governments have imposed four years of rent 
reductions and one year with a below inflation rent cap.  Although there is 
a 10-year rent standard in place, with annual increases of CPI +1%, there 
is a potential risk that the Government could amend this and impose further 
reductions, freezes or capped increases which will significantly harm the 
HRA’s ability to fund its revenue and capital activities. 
 
f) There have been instances where the Council has been unable to charge 
leaseholders for major works undertaken as a result of s20 billing issues or 
where the Council has billed and been successfully challenged in court.  
The HRA business plan assumes the use of s20 income to contribute 

=== PDF PAGE 13 ===
towards the cost of its stock investment programme and reducing this 
contribution could increase the need for additional borrowing. 
g) There remains a significant risk that emerging and ongoing legislative 
requirements including Awaab’s Law, Minimum Energy Efficiency 
Standards, Building and Fire Safety regulations, and obligations to maintain 
decency across the stock, will place further pressure on the existing stock 
investment programme. While additional investment has been included in 
this budget to mitigate and address these requirements, compliance may 
still necessitate increased spending on repairs, maintenance, and the 
management of disrepair claims, potentially impacting the affordability and 
deliverability of the programme. 
 
h) All agreed Revenue savings will need to be delivered to maintain a 
balanced position.  The delivery of these savings will need to be closely 
monitored as slippage or non-delivery will result in unbudgeted expenditure 
which the HRA will need to be able to absorb. 
 
2.36 To mitigate these risks, the HRA Business Plan is updated annually to reflect 
changes in assumptions and ensure ongoing affordability. The impact of new 
capital schemes, as well as any slippage within existing programmes, is 
modelled throughout the year and informs decision-making. There is 
also small headroom in the early years of the HRA Business Plan, which helps 
to offset the potential worsening of key assumptions. In addition, regular 
budget monitoring enables early identification of cost and income pressures, 
allowing timely mitigating actions to be developed and implemented. 
 
 
Adequacy of Reserves   
 
2.37 Determining the appropriate levels of reserves and balances is a matter 
professional judgement based on local circumstances including the overall 
budget size, risks, robustness of budgets, major initiatives being undertaken, 
budget assumptions and the Council's track record in budget management.  
 
2.38 Under the 2003 Act, the Secretary of State has reserve powers to set a 
minimum level of reserves. The most likely use of this power is where an 
authority is running down its reserves against the advice of their Statutory S151 
Officer. 
 
2.39 The Section 151 Officer will ultimately exercise control over the prudent use of 
all reserves. It should be noted that the consequences of not keeping a 
minimum prudent level of reserves and balances can have extremely serious 
impacts on a local authority and can result in the Section 151 Officer to seek 
Exceptional Finance Support from the Government.  
 
2.40 CIPFA provide advice to Chief Financial Officers in forming their views on the 
robustness of reserves and resilience of reserves and point to the relative 
position of an authority in comparison to a cohort of similar sized authorities 
with comparable demographic composition. The data for the Financial

Resilience Index is obtained from the revenue expenditure and financing 
England outturn report ('RO forms') and the capital outturn return (‘COR C’) 
2024-25. It reflects figures submitted by local authorities to MHCLG, published 
in December 2025.

b) Change in Reserves – This indicator represents the change in reserves 
(total useable excluding public health and schools) over the past three

2.42 The CIPFA Resilience Index shows that both the current level of reserves and 
the recent change in reserves sit toward the higher‑risk end of the spectrum, 
reflecting the significant drawdown of reserves in recent years. This position is 
mainly due to the substantial cost pressures the Council continues to face in 
homelessness and adult social care, where demand and market‑driven 
inflation have materially impacted financial performance. Taken together, 
these indicators highlight the need to strengthen the Council’s financial 
resilience. The planned rebuilding of reserves over the MTFS period is 
therefore essential to restoring a more sustainable financial position and 
ensuring the Council remains able to respond to ongoing volatility in demand, 
pricing and service pressures. 
 
2.43 The use of reserves is not intended to replace savings or income generation 
within the MTFS. Reserves can only be used once, and relying on them to 
avoid delivering savings or securing new income merely delays the issue and 
creates additional pressures in future years. Where reserves are earmarked 
for a specific purpose, their use reflects the delivery of that purpose and is not 
problematic. The General Fund balance and the Risk Reserve are the two 
principal reserves that support the budget, providing resilience against 
financial uncertainty and helping to maintain a stable underlying budget 
position.

2.52 The Contingency within the annual Budget will be used in the first instance to 
meet the cost of risks. The in-year contingency budget has been increased by 
£5m to £10m in 2026-27. The Council's unrestricted usable Earmarked 
Revenue Reserves act as a second line of defence. This contingency and 
reserves provide headroom in relation to any budget overspends, and these 
measures cannot fully mitigate the extent of the risks over the Medium Term.  
The General Fund balance is the absolute last line of defence and a 
requirement to utilise this balance would indicate severe financial difficulty. 
 
2.53 The Council cannot rely on its reserves to cover future funding gaps, and so 
senior officers and members must deliver on the savings that have been 
identified and continue to manage in year pressures within the financial 
envelope. 
 
2.54 The minimum level of balances cannot be judged merely against the current 
risks facing the Council as these can and will change over time. Reserves must 
remain adequate over the medium to long term.  
 
 
Conclusion 
 
2.55 In conclusion, having considered the risks, assumptions and uncertainties set 
out in this statement, I am satisfied that the budget estimates for 2026-27 are 
sufficiently robust for budget‑setting purposes and Council Tax, and that the 
General Fund balance and reserves, though low, are adequate for 2026-27 
given the financial pressures facing the Council. 
 
2.56 It is therefore imperative that the Council rebuilds and strengthens the reserves 
as proposed in this budget. However, it is essential to recognise that both the 
2026-27 position and the medium‑term financial outlook remain exposed to 
substantial and increasing sector wide risks impacting all other local 
authorities. Continued vigilance will be critical. The Council must closely 
monitor its financial position, take timely and decisive action to address 
emerging risks, and progress the strengthening of reserves as set out in this 
budget to ensure the long‑term sustainability and resilience of its finances.
