SUMMARY 
 
The Budget and Medium-Term Financial Strategy (MTFS) forms part of a wider 
framework of strategies that guide how the council delivers its statutory duties, achieves 
the priorities set out in the Council Plan, and maintains long term financial resilience. 
The Medium-Term Financial Strategy explains how the council intends to allocate its 
resources over the next four years to support these activities within the funding it 
expects to receive. 
 
This report outlines how the council’s 2026/27 Budget and MTFS will enable progress 
toward its vision, despite rising demand for, and cost of, its services. 
 
The council is committed to strengthening community resilience and empowering local 
communities. The Council Plan sets out a strong ambition to adopt a community centred 
model, supporting families, neighbours, and wider communities to connect with one 
another and build the kinds of formal and informal support networks that have 
historically held communities together. Residents want to live in vibrant, connected 
places with a strong sense of pride and identity, and the council aims to use its 
resources to nurture community assets and the social connections that make this 
possible.

Approach to Budget and Medium-Term Financial Strategy 
 
1. The council’s Budget and Medium-Term Financial Strategy (MTFS) supports 
the Council Plan and the key priorities of the council over the MTFS period. 
2. The budget process is priority-led, aligning the allocation of resources with 
council priorities and ensuring the council can meet its statutory duties. The 
Council Plan includes three key priorities supported by nine priority outcomes 
covering the MTFS period: 
• Creating good jobs 
• Tackling the climate crisis 
• Fighting inequality. 
3. The aim of the MTFS is to ensure a stable and sustainable financial position 
that will allow the council to achieve its vision and strategic objectives. It 
reflects the impact of government funding decisions and the wider national and 
local economic context. It provides a robust financial framework to support the 
achievement of the council’s overall objectives and delivery of services to its 
residents.  
4. The MTFS is developed based upon the following principles: 
• Robust budget setting, taking account of known pressures and current 
expenditure levels, prepared in consultation with Strategic Directors.  
• Regular monitoring of budgets and robust management and mitigating 
actions to address unplanned variances that arise in-year, reporting to 
the Strategic Leadership Team (SLT) and Cabinet. 
• Appropriate levels of income with effective debt management and 
monitoring to ensure prompt collection. 
• Prudent assessment of future resources and cost pressures. 
• Plans are put in place for all savings proposals and delivery is monitored. 
• Maximisation of external grant funding to meet priorities. 
• Prudent assessment of provisions required to mitigate future liabilities. 
• Risk assessed level of reserves and balances to mitigate potential 
financial liabilities, meet commitments and enable investment in 
transformation and change to deliver future savings and manage risk. 
• Prudent and planned use of reserves to fund one-off expenditure. 
• Effective forecasting and management of the council’s cash flow 
requirements, and effective management of treasury management risks 
and opportunities. 
• Integration of revenue and capital decisions, to ensure that the revenue 
implications of capital projects are reflected in the MTFS. 
• Prudent and proportionate use of borrowing for capital investment that is 
not funded by capital receipts or contributions from third parties. 
5. The 2026/27 budget has been set in the context of the government rebuilding 
the relationship with local government via the Spending Review and Funding

Reform. Over the past year, the government consulted on reforms to the local 
government funding system, building on the 2025/26 Settlement. The 
government released three related consultations: Funding Reform Objectives 
and Principles, Fair Funding Review 2.0 (FFR2.0), and Resetting the Business 
Rates Retention System. The 2026/27 local government finance settlement is 
the first year of a three-year settlement to 2028/29. Local authorities continue 
to face significant demand and market pressures driving above-inflation 
increases in costs. For a significant number of local authorities these 
pressures have proved unmanageable within their available resources, with 30 
councils requiring exceptional financial support in 2025/26. 
6. The approach in setting the budget has been to limit or mitigate growth 
wherever possible while continuing to provide quality statutory services and to 
maintain service standards. The nature and the extent of pressures being 
experienced in demand-led services has meant, however, that significant 
growth has been included in the budget to meet these pressures and reflect 
current and expected expenditure in these areas. This has been mitigated 
wherever possible through the following actions: 
• Robust identification of pressures, prepared in consultation with Strategic 
Directors. 
• Review of fees and charges, ensuring income is maximised through 
charges to, and income from residents and third parties benefiting from 
the services, ensuring that costs are covered. 
• Mitigation and management measures, such as detailed reviews of 
placements and other demand and market management. 
• Review of corporate budgets, including treasury management. 
• Directorate and service-led transformation and savings proposals over 
the MTFS period. 
7. In addition, the administration has chosen to invest in a number of universal 
services to residents which have historically been squeezed in response to 
funding reductions. These include investments in community safety, streets, 
playgrounds and leisure facilities for young people.  
National context - UK Finances 
8. The UK government’s fiscal rules that took effect in 2025 are designed to 
restore economic stability and support sustainable growth. They form a central 
component of the government’s strategy to ensure the sustainable 
management of public finances, while supporting long-term economic growth.  
By prioritising capital investment over day-to-day spending, the framework is 
designed to strengthen economic resilience, improve productivity, and secure 
better outcomes for public services. This approach aims to create the 
conditions for sustained recovery, enhance the UK’s competitiveness, and 
support delivery of the government’s strategic objectives.

National context - Local Government Finances 
15. Local government has faced well over a decade of reduced funding and rising 
demand for council services. As local authorities faced growing demands and 
reduced funding over the last decade, concerns about their financial 
sustainability have risen. The number of councils seeking exceptional support 
from central government has surged from three in 2019/20 to 30 in 2025/26. 
16. As set out to the Overview and Scrutiny Committee on 6 November 2025, the 
Fair Funding Review (FFR) 2.0 represents a significant shift towards a more 
equitable and transparent funding system for local authorities in England. FFR 
2.0 aims to create a fairer, evidence-based system that directs resources to 
areas with higher deprivation and need.  
17. The Key Objectives of FFR 2.0 are: 
• Equitable Funding Distribution: funding will be directed toward areas 
with higher deprivation and need, correcting imbalances created by 
previous systems that disadvantaged less affluent authorities. 
• Multi-Year Settlements: the government will introduce multi-year Local 
Government Finance Settlements for the first time in a decade, providing 
greater stability and predictability. 
• Simplified Funding Formulas: the review reduces the number of needs 
assessment formulas from 15 to nine, making the allocation process 
clearer and more responsive to local circumstances. 
• Addressing Historical Inequities: the new approach aims to fix long 
standing reliance on council tax and business rates, ensuring funding 
better reflects real service demands and local economic conditions. 
National context - Local Government Finance Settlement 
18. Following consultation on FFR 2.0 and the Local Government Policy 
Statement on 20 November 2025 which set out how FFR 2.0 would be 
delivered, the 2026/27 Provisional Local Government Finance Settlement 
(PLGFS) was announced by the Secretary of State on 17 December 2025, 
and at the time of writing, the Final Settlement is awaited. The government 
published the first multi-year Local Government Finance Settlement in a 
decade. The main headlines are:  
• Fair Funding Assessment (FFA): the PLGFS reallocates 2025/26 funding 
into the Baseline Funding Level (BFL) and Revenue Support Grant 
(RSG), with increases applied through indexing. The FFA also 
incorporates funding for new responsibilities under the Simpler Recycling 
initiative.

• Business Rates Reset and Reform: the division of funding into BFL and 
RSG reflects changes from the business rates revaluation, reset, and 
new multipliers. Although the reform does not alter overall redistribution, 
faster national growth in BFL compared with RSG may increase funding 
for councils, alongside increased collection risk. 
• Council Tax: For 2026/27, referendum limits remain at 2.99% plus 2% for 
social care authorities, with higher thresholds for shire districts, fire 
authorities, police authorities, and the GLA. Six councils will face no 
referendum limits in 2027/28 and 2028/29, enabling unrestricted council 
tax increases. These councils are: City of London, Hammersmith and 
Fulham, Kensington and Chelsea, Wandsworth, Westminster and 
Windsor and Maidenhead.  
• Adult Social Care Funding: Adult social care allocations are redistributed 
through the FFA, with the Local Authority Better Care Grant (LABCG) 
issued as a section 31 grant. Additional funding of £150m in 2026/27, 
rising to £500m in 2028/29, will be added to RSG using a new relative 
needs formula that excludes council tax considerations. The Department 
of Health and Social Care will publish indicative amounts to guide 
expected expenditure. 
• Funding Floor and Transitional Costs: transitional protection totals 
£146.9m in 2026/27, increasing to £567.5m by 2028/29, indicating that 
full transition to the new funding model will not be achieved within the 
initial three-year settlement period. Local Government Reorganisation 
may reduce this requirement from 2029/30. 
• Recovery Grant: the 2025/26 Recovery Grant will continue, with some 
authorities receiving increases designed to raise Core Spending Power 
(CSP) by 5%, 6%, and 7% over the three years, capped at £35m per 
authority. 
19. The broad policy approach set out in the provisional settlement reflects: 
• Greater share of resources towards councils with higher levels of 
deprivation and a lower ability to raise council tax locally. 
• Implementing FFR 2.0 for 2026/27 which includes updated Relative 
Needs Formulae with refreshed population, deprivation and service 
specific indicators. 
• Consolidation and simplification of grants, including the rolling‑in of 
multiple Children’s Services grants into a single Children, Families and 
Youth Grant, and the transfer of certain business rates related grants into 
Revenue Support Grant (RSG) as part of the 2026 rates reset. 
• Additional funding for priority services, namely Adult Social Care, 
Children’s Social Care and for homelessness. 
• An assumed increase in council tax up to a 2.99% threshold, and a 
further 2% social care levy.

22. A local authority’s Fair Funding Allocation (FFA) is determined through a 
complex, formula-based assessment of relative need and local resources. In

simplified terms, the methodology calculates funding based on assessed need 
and then deducts an amount reflecting the authority’s capacity to raise 
resources locally. This process underpins how the government equalises 
funding distribution through the FFA, which serves as the core mechanism for 
allocating grants and business rates. 
23. The FFA is split into Business Rates retention through the Baseline Funding 
Level (BFL) and Revenue Support Grant (RSG). The 2026/27 business rate 
scheme contains significant changes including: 
• A full reset aligning NNDR baselines with expected 2026/27 collections. 
• Transition from net to gross rates system. 
• Introduction of new multipliers for Retail, Hospitality & Leisure sectors 
funded by a High-Value Multiplier. 
• Revised levy and safety net arrangements with a 100% safety net in 
2026/27, decreasing to 97% and 92.5% in subsequent years depending 
on business rates pooling pilot status. 
• Adjustments for appeals and bad debts factored nationally. 
24. For council tax, the government has mostly kept to the referendum limits from 
previous years. Social care authorities are allowed an increase of 2.99% plus 
a social care precept of up to 2% without a referendum, and this is assumed in 
calculating CSP. 
25. Where a council is deemed by government to be in need of exceptional 
financial support, and additional council tax increases are critical to 
maintaining their financial sustainability, the government will consider requests 
for bespoke referendum principles. These local proposals will be considered 
on a case-by-case basis. Six councils will face no referendum limits in 2027/28 
and 2028/29, enabling unrestricted council tax increases. These councils are 
City of London, Hammersmith and Fulham, Kensington and Chelsea, 
Wandsworth, Westminster and Windsor and Maidenhead.

Council Tax Empty Property Premium 
49. From April 2013, the government introduced legislation allowing authorities to 
charge a premium on top of the normal council tax for dwellings that have 
been empty and substantially unfurnished for at least two years (excluding 
those exempted from paying such as those under probate, or where 
occupation is prohibited by law). This allowed the council to introduce a 
premium of 50% to be added to the council tax bill. The drive behind this was 
to reduce the number of properties left empty for long periods of time and 
bring these back into use, particularly given the challenges of housing 
shortages. 
50. In 2018, the government announced the extension of the empty property 
premium to double the amount of the premium to be levied and allowed 
authorities to charge a 100% additional amount from 1 April 2019. This charge 
could be levied on all long-term empty properties that had been empty for 
more than 2 years. Full Council on 26 February 2019 agreed to charge a 
100% premium (increased from 50%) on top of the standard council tax for 
properties which have been empty for more than 2 years, with effect from 1 
April 2019. 
51. Further to this change, the government introduced legislation for this premium 
to be extended to allow authorities to charge a 200% premium from April 2020 
for properties empty for over 5 years, and then a 300% premium from April 
2021 for properties empty for over 10 years. From April 2020, Full Council 
decided that in addition to the 100% premium being charged on properties 
empty for over 2 years, to charge a further 100% (300% in total) for those 
properties empty for more than 5 years. 
52. From 1 April 2021, Full Council further extended this charge in line with 
legislation with a 300% premium to be charged (400% in total) for all qualifying 
properties empty for more than 10 years. 
53. The government then amended the legislation for empty properties such that 
the 100% premium (where owners are charged double the normal council tax) 
for properties empty over 2 years would be chargeable after 1 year only. This 
would increase pressure on owners of these properties to ensure these homes 
are brought back into use as soon as possible. This change was introduced 
from 1 April 2024. 
54. Additionally, under this new legislation councils could charge a 100% premium 
on second homes (where the property is not occupied as someone’s sole or 
main residence but is furnished) from 1 April 2025. The council made this 
determination on 5 March 2024 which took effect from 1 April 2025. 
55. A series of exceptions to these premiums were introduced from 1 April 2025.  
These are listed in the Council Tax (Prescribed Classes of Dwellings and

Council Tax Reduction Scheme 
57. Council tax reduction (CTR) caseload is divided into two main categories: 
pensioners and working age claimants. The pensioner category applies to 
those who have reached state pension retirement age. This category is 
protected, and council tax reduction is paid according to the regulations which 
are prescribed by central government. The Ealing CTR scheme for working 
age residents is a local scheme and the council is responsible for setting out 
eligibility criteria for the scheme.  
58. The full scheme is presented in Appendix 4. In summary, the scheme operates 
with four key features. 
59. Key Feature 1: is the entitlement to CTR based on income bands and on the 
net income of the claimant and their partner (if they live with one). The banded 
scheme provides a maximum 100% reduction for claimants who fall into the 
protected category, and up to 80% for all other claimants.  
60. Under the income banded scheme, the calculation of entitlement to CTR is 
much simpler than under a means-tested calculation. All state benefits 
including universal credit are disregarded from the calculation, and claimants 
receive a discount based upon the level of their net income. The income 
bands are uprated in line with September CPI on an annual basis. Income 
bands for 2026/27 have been uprated by 3.8%.

Contributions to Reserves 
102. The financial environment for local authorities remains highly challenging, 
reflected in the increasing number of councils requiring Exceptional Financial 
Support. With continued pressures from rising demand and escalating costs, it 
is essential that councils regularly assess their financial resilience and ability 
to respond to external risks. 
103. To support financial stability, councils are advised to maintain sufficient 
reserves and balances to mitigate unexpected funding losses or cost 
pressures. In line with the agreed strategy, the base budget includes an 
annual contribution of £3.5m to strengthen reserves. Although progress 
continues to be made and balances are in a healthier position overall, the 
council’s reserve levels remain comparatively average among London 
boroughs. The Strategic Director, Resources considers this approach 
appropriate given the growing volatility in demand-led services and the trends 
observed in the 2025/26 quarterly financial monitoring. Strengthening reserves 
provides protection against funding uncertainty, income losses, and in-year 
pressures, while ensuring resources are available for future transformation 
initiatives.

Adequacy of Reserves and Balances  
176. Under the 2003 Local Government Act, the Strategic Director, Resources is 
required to report on the adequacy of proposed financial reserves. 
177. There is no statutory definition of an adequate level of reserves, which is a 
matter of judgement for the Chief Finance Officer. In coming to a judgement 

=== PDF PAGE 44 ===
44
 
 
on this matter the Strategic Director, Resources has considered: 
• The robustness of estimates contained within the budget and risks to the 
budget strategy, as set out above. 
• Current and past financial performance against budget. 
• The effectiveness of financial controls and budget monitoring procedures, 
and the ability of the council’s leadership to respond to budget variations. 
178. The council’s General Fund balance is the un-earmarked reserve held for 
unavoidable cost increases and significant unexpected events which cannot 
be reasonably foreseen. It acts as a financial safety net for a council to 
continue to deliver essential public services whilst any necessary corrective 
action is planned and taken. The General Fund balance should not have 
commitments against it, and should not be relied upon to enable the setting 
of a balanced budget.  
179. Whilst there is no formal guidance on the level of the General Fund balance, 
it has been generally accepted that it should, at a minimum, represent 5% of 
an authority’s net revenue budget.  However, the volatility experienced since 
2020 in relation to the pandemic and the impact of wider economic and geo-
political factors, such as the Ukraine war and continuing conflict in the Middle 
East, in relation to demand and local authority costs, suggest that this 
previous benchmark may be out of date, and authorities should consider 
whether this benchmark continues to be adequate.  
180. The council’s General Fund balance of £20.471m is within the Strategic 
Director, Resource’s target risk-assessed level of between £17.5m and 
£25m. There is a planned contribution in the base budget to increase 
unearmarked reserves. A General Fund balance of £20.471m is 6% of the 
total net budget for 2025/26 of £323.476m. The Strategic Director, Resources 
considers that a forecast balance of £20.471m at 31 March 2026 is adequate 
given the risks the council is facing, and considering the council’s recent 
spending history and balanced outturn positions. This is the middle rather 
than the top of the range and the Strategic Director, Resources will consider, 
in consultation with members, at 2025/26 outturn whether there are 
opportunities to move this towards the top of the range given the increasingly 
volatile, uncertain, complex and ambiguous environment in which local 
authorities operate.  
181. In assessing the adequacy of financial reserves, the Strategic Director, 
Resources needs to consider all the reserves and balances available to the 
council. The council holds a number of reserves to manage risks, such as the 
collection fund equalisation reserve and insurance reserve, and those to 
meet known future commitments such as PFI reserves. Earmarked reserves 
reduce over the medium-term as the sums built up in these are deployed, as 
shown in Appendix 10. These specific reserves are considered sufficient to 

=== PDF PAGE 45 ===

manage the risks and liabilities for which they are earmarked.  
182. Historically, the council had allowed the reserves balances to dip to an 
unsustainable level, utilising the reserves to balance annual budgets year-on-
year. To rectify this, the council agreed in 2021/22 a reserves strategy to 
include a base budget annual contribution of £3.5m to reserves to build back 
financial reserves to provide protection against the financial uncertainty of 
local government funding, substantial losses in core income and in-year 
demand and market pressures. This contribution is made to the Economic 
Volatility Reserve.  
183. Similarly, over the last few years the HRA has utilised its reserves to balance 
its budget. The HRA Business Plan for 2025/26 started to replenish the 
reserves lost with a planned contribution to reserves after a £2m contingency 
budget, which will transfer to reserves if not used in 2025/26 and this will 
continue in 2026/27.  
184. Whilst external benchmarking shows that the council’s direction of travel on 
reserves has lowered the risk to the council, Ealing remains comparatively 
high-risk having average levels of reserves compared to other London 
boroughs. However a number of London Boroughs are in EFS and have had 
to draw significantly from their reserves to balance the budget and hold 
unsustainable levels of reserves. The experience of councils in EFS 
demonstrate the speed in which reserves can now be extinguished when 
councils are unable to balance their budget.  
185. The current service overspend in quarter 3 for 2025/26 of £19.1m, whilst 
largely offset by contingency and other one-off corporate budget 
underspends, gives an indication of the potential need to draw on reserves 
and the extent to which adequate reserves are needed in addition to the 
General Fund balance.  
186. Another local factor which is important in considering the adequacy of 
reserves, is the council’s comparatively high level of borrowing to fund 
historic capital expenditure. This results in high fixed costs of external interest 
and prudent minimum revenue provision payments. A higher-than-average 
proportion of the council’s expenditure is fixed, which leaves less flexibility to 
deliver short to medium-term savings in response to any deterioration in the 
financial position, and therefore reserves are needed to provide a cushion, 
and time to consider and implement more difficult savings options and 
strategies.
