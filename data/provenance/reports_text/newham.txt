Introduction by Cllr Zulfiqar Ali, Cabinet Member for Finance and Resource 
 
As Cabinet Lead for Finance and Resources, I am pleased to say there will be no 
Exceptional Financial Support (EFS) as we are presenting a Balanced Budget for the 
2026/27 financial year alongside a strengthened and more sustainable Medium-Term 
Financial Strategy for the years ahead. This is the final budget of this administration, and 
it represents an important milestone for our borough. It is not simply a financial document; 
it is a statement of resilience, responsibility and long-term sustainability. It reflects the 
collective effort, discipline and shared values of this Labour administration, our senior 
leadership team and the many dedicated council staff who work tirelessly for the people of 
Newham.  
 
Over recent years we have faced unprecedented financial and social pressures. Rising 
demand for essential services — particularly homelessness and temporary 
accommodation — the cost-of-living crisis, persistent inflation, and historic underfunding 
have created sustained strain on local government finances. Behind these pressures are 
real lives: families seeking secure housing, older residents needing dignity in care, young 
people searching for opportunity, and vulnerable residents requiring protection and 
support. Throughout these challenges, our guiding principle has been clear: financial 
responsibility must go hand in hand with compassion, and resilience must be built through 
sustainable decisions rather than short-term fixes. 
  
This administration has never shied away from difficult choices, but we have always 
approached them with residents at the centre of our thinking and with a firm commitment 
to financial resilience and organisational sustainability. Our priorities have been to protect 
frontline services wherever possible, strengthen neighbourhoods, invest in prevention and 
early intervention, support young people and families, and ensure that those most in need 
are not left behind. These priorities are not only socially responsible — they are financially 
sustainable, reducing future demand and building long-term resilience into the council’s 
service delivery model.  
 
We have faced unprecedented challenges in homelessness and Temporary 
Accommodation costs, along with rising costs of service delivery and demographic 
changes. Despite these we have made significant progress in improving financial 
governance, transparency and accountability. Stronger scrutiny processes and clearer 
reporting. These have increased organisational resilience and enabled more sustainable 
financial decision-making. These improvements are fundamental to ensuring that Newham 
is not simply reacting to pressures year by year but is building a stable and sustainable 
financial future.  
 
Balancing this Budget has required honesty, discipline and transformation. Through 
detailed Budget challenge sessions, zero-based budgeting reviews, benchmarking with 
other authorities and a relentless focus on value for money, we have examined every area 
of expenditure. This has not been about reducing services for the sake of savings; it has 
been about redesigning services, so they are efficient, modern and financially sustainable. 
We have placed strong emphasis on prevention, digital enablement and service redesign 
so that improvements today contribute to financial resilience tomorrow.  
 
Our Capital programme has also been carefully reviewed to ensure that investment 
supports long-term sustainability. We have prioritised safety, statutory obligations,

One of the most significant achievements of this administration has been restoring 
Financial Resilience. We have rebuilt General Fund Balance, strengthened internal 
controls, improved forecasting and adopted a prudent approach to borrowing. These 
measures are not merely technical financial actions; they are the foundations of 
sustainability. A resilient council is one that can withstand economic uncertainty, respond 
to unexpected demand and continue delivering reliable services to its residents. The 
financial position we present today is markedly stronger, more stable and more sustainable 
than in previous years.  
 
This Budget also introduces key proposals and improvements that directly support both 
social and financial sustainability. These include continued investment in housing supply 
and homelessness prevention to reduce long-term temporary accommodation pressures; 
strengthened support for children and families through earlier intervention; sustainable 
funding for adult social care; targeted improvements to neighbourhood and environmental 
services; and focused assistance for residents facing cost-of-living challenges. Alongside 
these service investments, we continue to advance digital transformation and modern 
service delivery, improving accessibility for residents while delivering efficiencies that 
enhance long-term financial sustainability.  
 
Importantly, this Budget reflects a shift toward more preventative and demand-
management approaches. By investing in early help, community support and service 
redesign, we are building resilience into the system itself — reducing avoidable costs, 
improving outcomes and creating a more sustainable service model for the future. These 
changes are not short-term savings; they are structural improvements designed to 
strengthen the council’s financial and operational sustainability over the medium and long 
term.  
 
We also recognise the importance of the national funding context. Emerging reforms and 
fairer funding considerations have begun to better acknowledge the scale of need in 
boroughs such as Newham. While prudent Financial Management remains essential, this 
evolving landscape provides a more stable planning environment and contributes to the 
improved medium-term outlook we are now able to present — further reinforcing the 
Resilience and Sustainability of our Financial strategy.  
 
As this is the final Budget of this administration, it is right to recognise those who have 
made this progress possible. I extend sincere thanks to the Mayor, my Cabinet colleagues, 
Scrutiny Members, all elected Members, our senior leadership team, the Section 151 
Officer, our finance professionals and every member of staff who has worked with 
professionalism and dedication, often under considerable pressure. I also thank the 
residents and businesses of Newham for their engagement, feedback and trust. Their 
voices have strengthened our decision-making and ensured that Financial Resilience and 
Sustainability remain central to our approach.  
 
We leave the council’s finances on a far more secure and sustainable footing than in 
previous years. This Balanced Budget provides stability, resilience and a credible long-

1. Executive Summary 
 
1.1. As per the Local Government Finance Act 1992, the Executive is required to 
recommend to Council for approval a balanced Revenue Budget alongside a 
Capital Programme on an annual basis before 11 March. 
 
1.2. The main purpose of this report is to enable the Mayor and Cabinet to consider 
its final 2026/27 Revenue Budget proposals and the proposed Capital 
Programme at a formal meeting of Cabinet on the 17 February 2026, 
considering the final Local Government Financial Settlement, which was 
published by the Ministry of Housing Communities and Local Government 
(MHCLG) on the 9 February 2026. 
 
1.3. Thereafter, the Executive will present its recommendations to Council to 
approve a final 2026/27 Revenue Budget, the Capital Programme to 2028/29 
and set the Council Tax for the 2026/27 financial year at a meeting of Full 
Council on 27 February 2026. The budget is based on an increase of 4.99% in 
Council Tax, made up of a 2.99% general Council Tax increase, and a 2.00% 
Adult Social Care Precept increase. 
 
1.4. This report explains the changes made to the 2026/27 draft ‘Building a Fairer 
Newham through Financial Sustainability’ Budget published on the 22 
December 2025 which was approved by the Mayor and Cabinet on 8 January 
2026 and subject to further engagement with residents, stakeholders and 
discussions with the Council’s Scrutiny Budget Working Committee. 
 
1.5. In summary the final 2026/27 Budget proposals balances; significant ongoing 
demand led, demographic and inflationary pressures of £59.860m; with total 
savings of £55.747m; and additional income, mainly because of the 
government’s Fair Funding Review increases of £44.293m. A balanced outturn 
position requires the delivery of all the savings set in this report to time, value 
and profile. 
 
1.6. As previously highlighted in the draft 2026/27 Budget report presented to the 
Mayor and Cabinet in January 2026, Exceptional Financial Support in the form 
of using Capital receipts to achieve a balanced Revenue position is not 
required from central government to balance the proposed 2026/27 Final 
Budget plan. This means that the Council will not be selling assets to fund the 
ongoing costs of delivering services, as it now has a sustainable Balanced 
Budget for the next 3 years, and a sustainable approach to a new 5-year 
Medium Term Financial Strategy approach.  
 
1.7. The Council has addressed Financial Sustainability in the 2026/27 Budget 
and MTFS and will continue this Financially Sustainable position, balancing its 
Budget in a sustainable way for 3 years – 2026/27, 2027/28 and 2028/29.  This 
budget demonstrates the improvement made to establish Financial 
Sustainability and stability as required by MHCLG in its non-statutory Best 
Value Notice to the authority in May 20251.  The notice included following:

3. Background 
 
3.1. This report follows the approval of the draft 2026/27 Budget proposals, for the 
purposes of engagement, in a report to Cabinet in January 2025. That report 
set out in detail the budget proposals regarding savings and growth as well as 
all income assumptions, which resulted in a Balanced Budget being proposed 
for 2026/27, without recourse to Exceptional Financial Support (EFS). This 
report updates those proposals, detailing all changes being made to those 
proposals. 
 
3.2. This report also follows the presentation of the Housing Revenue Account 
Business plan to Cabinet in December 2025, and a Housing Revenue Account 
Budget Setting report to Cabinet earlier on this same agenda. 
 
3.3. Fair Funding Review 
 
3.4. The draft 2026/27 Budget report described how there has been a major change 
in government funding figures since the government responded through its 
‘Local government finance policy statement 2026-27 to 2028-29 ’ published 20 
November 2025, to the Fair Funding 2.0 Review consultation; and the 
subsequent release of its provisional funding figures for 2026/27 on the 17 
December 2025. These figures have been confirmed by the Final Local 
Government Finance Settlement announced 9 February 2026, with only a 
relatively minor adjustment which is explained further down in this report. 
 
3.5. Under its Fair Funding 2.0 approach, the government is allocating resources to 
local councils based on a series of formula, all of which are calculated from 
appropriate data sources. 

=== PDF PAGE 11 ===
Appendix AA 
 
 
 
3.6. In summary, the main changes made by government between what was 
originally consulted on and the formal response to that consultation, relate to 
how the government will:  
 
 Use the most up to date population data available, rather than relying on 
Census data taken during the Covid-19 pandemic; 
 Update the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) data so that deprivation is 
central to government grant allocations, including the cost of Housing; and 
 Update the Children’s element of the funding formula so that it uses the 
most up to date Income Deprivation Affecting Children Index (IDACI) data. 
 
3.7. Modelling from London Councils shows how these changes will impact London 
Boroughs, with the London Borough of Newham now scoring over 50% more 
highly on the update IMD deprivation data than previously, and over 80% more 
highly on the IDACI data than previously – see Charts 1 and 2 below.

3.13. Homelessness – The Council continues to experience high demand for the 
provision of temporary accommodation following the cost of living and housing 
crisis. 
 
3.14. Adult Social Care  – Increasing demographic and complexity pressures on 
demand and escalation of care need in Adult Social Cre. 
 
3.15. Children’s Social Care – Increased complexity of need driving cost increases

3.16. Inflation – the general rate of increase in the cost of goods and services is

3.17. Levies – population growth and demographic change is driving the increases in

the Newham share of Transport for London’s Freedom Pass scheme. 
 
3.18. Internal Investment  – the council needs to invest in its transformation of 
operations, in the provision of data and information technology services, and in 
its internal control and procurement functions. 
 
3.19. Risks 
 
3.20. National and economic risks impacting the budget and Financial Strategy 
include the following: 
 
3.21. Economic growth  – The inability to attract and retain investment, stimulate 
local business growth, and implement economic development strategies will 
result in stagnant or declining economic performance, reduced employment 
opportunities, and diminished public revenue, ultimately impacting the quality of 
local public services and increased financial instability. 
 
3.22. Economic Risks – Increasing costs due to inflation and demographic pressures 
resulting in significant savings requirements in future years. A separate 
provision has also been set against the risk of fluctuations in the cost of energy 
supply, such as has been experienced in recent years. 
 
3.23. Interest rates  – risks of adverse movements on rates, both in terms of 
borrowing and investments. The risk of a sudden rise in the rate of inflation; the 
latest Retail Price Index is currently at a level above the Bank of England’s target 
of 2% per annum. Whilst inflation allocations in the budget reflect the current 
trends, we have seen in recent years global and macroeconomic events cause 
a steep increase in the rate of inflation which would feed through into higher 
costs of pay, supplies and services and contracts, as well as impacting the cost 
of borrowing to fund the Capital Programme. Whilst much of the Council’s debt 
is fixed rate, the requirement to replace maturing loans exposes the Council to 
interest rate volatility.

5. Building out the MTFS to 5 years 
 
5.1. Appendix R(i) shows the MTFS over a five year period whereas previously it

and five has been to make broad assumptions and estimates (listed below), and 
these will be refined in future versions of the MTFS. 
 
5.2. Beyond the core MTFS period, the Council has modelled a set of broad, high-
level financial assumptions for 2029/30 and 2030/31. These assumptions are 
intended to provide an indication of the potential scale of financial pressures 
facing the Council in the later years of the plan and should be regarded as 
illustrative planning assumptions rather than detailed forecasts. 
 
5.3. Inflationary pressures are assumed to continue into the later years, adding 
£12.000m in 2029/30 and £12.500m in 2030/31. This reflects the ongoing 
impact of contract inflation, service cost growth and residual price pressures, 
even where national inflation is expected to moderate over the medium term. 
 
5.4. Temporary Accommodation (TA) remains the most significant driver of cost 
pressures in the later years of the plan. Additional pressures of £20.500m in 
2029/30 and £17.900m in 2030/31 have been assumed. While the profile 
assumes some reduction in growth by 2030/31, TA costs remain highly 
sensitive to demand levels, market rents and wider housing market conditions. 
 
5.5. Demographic and demand-led pressures across statutory services are 
assumed to add a further £9.500m in 2029/30 and £10.000m in 2030/31, 
reflecting population growth, increasing complexity of need and cost pressures 
in key demand-led services. 
 
5.6. These pressures are partially offset by assumed improvements in funding and 
local income. Government income is assumed to increase by £8.700m in 
2029/30 and £8.900m in 2030/31, while growth in the Council Tax Base is 
assumed to generate additional income of £3.000m per annum in each year.

8. Capital Investment Strategy 
 
8.1. The draft budget report set out the approach to reviewing and amending the 
capital programme for the 2026/27 budget setting reports. This approach is 
summarised briefly below, and the full Capital Investment Strategy is provided 
as Appendix B. 
 
8.2. Prospective Schemes are initially considered against the following principles 
which the Mayor and Cabinet previously approved as part of the MTFS in the 
February 2024 Budget setting reports: 
 Alignment to the delivery of Building a Fairer Newham outcomes and the 
Local Plan, and that supports growth. 
 Schemes that have a strong financial case 
 Business Critical Schemes that minimise risk and enable operational 
efficiency (i.e. Health & Safety / ICT) 
 Schemes that are ready to go and deliverable from 2025/26 
 
8.3. For 2026/27, with regards to the financial position, including the council’s debt 
burden, greater emphasis was then placed on: 
 Schemes with a strong financial case which are effectively self-financing / 
can contribute towards delivery of revenue savings through a return on 
investment (ROI) whereby the financial benefit derived from the scheme 
meets or exceeds the total cost of the project including the borrowing costs 
over the lifetime of the asset.  
 Schemes which serve a business-critical need such as Health & Safety.  
 Schemes which attract external investment are also considered.  
 
8.4. There is a key interrelationship between revenue and capital budgets which 
places constraints on the size of the financial envelope available to fund further 
capital investment without the need to find savings elsewhere in Council

24.5. Legal Implications 
 
24.6. The Council is required under Part 1, Chapter III of the Local Government and 
Finance Act 1992 (the 1992 Act) to set a council tax for the forthcoming year 
and confirm its budget estimates. In accordance with Section 30(6) of the 1992 
Act, the decision must be made before 11 March of the preceding year (i.e. by 
midnight on 10 March) but is not invalid merely because it is made on or after 
that date. 
 
24.7. Each Member has a positive duty to ensure that the Council complies with its 
legal obligations, to set a lawfully balanced budget. In doing so, the Council’s 
prospective expenditure must not be likely to exceed its resources available to 
meet that expenditure.  
 
24.8.  The Chief Finance Officer has a legal duty to report to the Authority on the 
robustness of the estimates made for the purposes of the calculations and the 
adequacy of the proposed financial reserves. Cabinet must consider the Chief 
Finance Officer’s advice in this regard, when determining whether the proposals 
should be recommended to Council for approval. 
 
24.9. The approval of the budget and calculation of council tax is a decision reserved 
to (Full) Council under Section 67(2)(b) of the 1992 Act. Should Council seek to 
reject or vary the budget in any material respect (in a way which is contrary to 
the 43 Executive’s recommended budget), then the statutory process set out in 
Part 4.3 of the Constitution must be followed. 
 
24.10. Cabinet and Council, in recommending and then setting the Council Tax, must 
have due regard to the impact those decisions will have upon the Council’s 
statutory public sector equality duty as set out below. 
 
24.11. Equalities Implications 
 
24.12. The Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) under Section 149 of the Equality Act 
2010 requires the Council, when exercising its functions, to have due regard to: 
 the need to eliminate discrimination, harassment, victimisation and any 
other conduct that is prohibited by or under the Equality Act 2010;  
 the need to advance equality of opportunity between persons who share 
protected characteristics and those who do not, and;  
 foster good relations between those who have protected characteristics 
and those who do not. 
 
24.13. ‘Protected characteristics’ are: age, gender, race, disability, sexual orientation, 
marriage and civil partnerships, religion or belief, pregnancy and maternity and 
gender reassignment. The Council also recognises socio-economic 
disadvantage, care experience and health and wellbeing status as additional 
characteristics.  
 
24.14. Any budgetary decisions which subsequently need to be taken should be made 
in accordance with the duty set out above.
