Summary 
 
This report presents the Council’s proposed budgets for 2026/27, together with the latest 
Medium Term Financial Strategy forecasts to 2030/31. 
 
The annual budget process is the formal allocation that enables the delivery of the 
Council’s policies and priorities and also complies with the Council’s statutory obligations 
in setting the Council Tax for the following financial year.  The delivery of the Council’s 
priorities of value-for-money and living within our means are key themes which underpin 
the proposed budgets. 
 
In line with statutory requirements, the Assembly is required to approve the proposed 
Band D Council Tax for 2026/27 and to note the Mayor’s GLA precept which will be added 
to this amount. 
 
Members should note that the proposed budget includes decisions taken by the Cabinet 
on 16 February 2026 in relation to the Fees and Charges 2026/27 report. 
 
Members are also presented with the revised two-year Capital Programme to 2027/28, 
together with the Council’s proposed Capital Budget for 2026/27.  The budget proposals 
have been developed alongside the Council’s revised Capital Strategy and Treasury 
Management Strategy which are presented as a separate agenda item. 
 
The Assembly is also asked to approve the HRA revenue and capital budgets for 2026/27 
as well as the Dedicated Schools Budget for 2026/27.

Reason(s) 
 
The Council has a legal obligation to set its Council Tax for the following financial year and 
approve an annual, balanced budget on the advice of the Council’s Section 151 Officer. 
The relevant legal provisions are set out in the Local Government Finance Act 1992. The 
setting of the budget is a function reserved to Full Council (Assembly) following 
recommendation for approval by Cabinet. 
 
The Local Government Act 2003 Section 25 sets a specific duty on a local authority’s 
Chief Financial Officer (S151 Officer) to make a statement on their opinion on the 
robustness of the budget proposals and the adequacy of reserves factoring in the financial 
risks that the Council will be facing over the next 12 months. 
 
The Council’s constitution requires the Assembly to be responsible for the adoption of the 
Council’s Budget and Policy Framework including the level of Council Tax, Revenue 
Budget and Capital Budget and Programme. Once a Budget or a Policy Framework is in 
place, it will be the responsibility of the Cabinet to implement it.

2. Background 
 
2.1. The proposed final budgets have been prepared under considerable financial and 
operational challenges faced by the Council during 2025/26. Macro-economic factors 
have continued to impact the Council’s finances considerably, with inflation leading to 
significant cost rises and increases in interest rates slowing down development 
activity. This, in turn, has an impact on the Council’s subsidiary companies. 
 
2.2. The Council welcomes the implementation of the Government’s Funding Reform, 
which now provides a revised funding framework intended to more accurately reflect 
both current and forecast local need. The borough’s demographics have changed 
significantly over recent years with an increasingly younger population but with a 
working age population that requires greater support across all our services 
especially social care. Through its Investment and Acquisition Strategy, the Council 
has significantly increased affordable housing supply within the borough.  However, 
due to the impact of the pandemic, downturn in the economy including historically 
higher levels of inflation and a consequent cost of living crisis, means more residents 
require access to Council services.  
 

=== PDF PAGE 5 ===
 
 
2.3. The financial sustainability of the whole of Local Government is under stress and this 
has been seen by a number of Local Authorities requesting Exceptional Financial 
Support in 2025/26, effectively signifying their inability to deliver a balanced budget, 
and many others warning that they are close to that position (please see the S151 
Officer’s Section 25 Statement, Appendix H). 
 
2.4. As is the case across the country, social care funding, particularly adult social care 
funding and its escalating costs, is a significant challenge for all local authorities. 
LBBD continues to see rises in requests for support and assessments, and significant 
changes in the nature of needs within that ASC care, from older to working age 
adults, and complexity of that care has increased over the last five years.  
 
2.5. Although inflation has begun to stabilise, overall costs remain significantly higher than 
in previous years. Delivering key services is costing significantly more due to the high 
levels of inflation and the considerable change in the Borough’s demographics with 
much greater complexity of need for users of council services, particularly social 
care. Whilst, in general the demand for social care is increasing moderately, the level 
of need and associated costs for existing and new service users is much higher and 
much more complex. 
 
2.6. The combination of these factors has led to significant challenges for the Council in 
setting a balanced budget for 2026/27. 
 
2.7. Looking at next year and the remainder of the MTFS, the Council is likely to be facing 
further inflationary pressures and has expectations of rising demand for services 
and/or changes in complexity of need, particularly for social care and housing 
provision. These financial risks are set out in section 18 below. 
 
2.8. To facilitate regeneration in the borough and the provision of much needed housing, 
the Council approved an Investment and Acquisition Strategy (IAS) in 2016. The IAS 
Strategy was established to be self-financing and had a target ambition of delivering 
a 5% revenue return to the Council, both of which are now at risk due to cost 
increases and completion delays. 
 
2.9. Rising interest rates impact on the Council’s borrowing costs and, in particular, on the 
ambitions of the Council’s Investment and Acquisition (IAS) Strategy. Since 2016 the 
Council has delivered over 2,500 units of housing in the borough and a further 400 
units are also under construction and due to complete over the MTFS period. 
 
2.10. An inherent aspect of the IAS, and regeneration more generally, is the length of time 
it takes from a decision being taken by Cabinet to proceed with a regeneration 
scheme and the actual delivery of that scheme which exposes the Council to the 
inherent risks of housing development. Unfortunately, global events in recent years 
have meant that both these risks have crystallised with significant increases in both 
construction costs and interest rates.  
 
2.11. The rise in interest rates and the high inflation costs within the construction sector 
have led to a number of schemes costing more than originally planned and new 
schemes unable to pass the viability assessments. Whilst this has led to a pause on 
new schemes, there will still be a requirement for the Council to borrow an additional 
£138m to complete the existing approved schemes.

2.16. Economic uncertainty has resulted in increasing homelessness and requirements for

3. MTFS 2026/27 to 2030/31 Principles and Assumptions 
 
3.1. The Medium-Term Financial Strategy (MTFS) sets out the Council’s high-level 
approach to financial planning and the management of its financial resources to 
underpin its continued financial sustainability but also to ensure that its financial 
resources are aligned with the Council’s Strategic plans. The MTFS should highlight 
all matters affecting its financial position and sustainability over the medium term as 
well as consider strategic objectives and constraints in finances which may impact on 
decision making. 
 
3.2. To do this effectively, a Council needs to be fully aware of its financial position, and to 
be successful the Strategy must be owned by the wider organisation and those 
responsible for decision-making. 
 
3.3. The Council’s assets should be a key feature of financial planning ensuring any value 
from those assets is maximised. The financial strategy should consider every 
opportunity to rationalise its asset base and/or improve returns on capital invested in 
those assets. For this reason, the Council’s Capital Strategy should sit alongside the 
Medium-Term Financial Strategy and consider future asset investment requirements 
and/or identify where required investment is not affordable and there is potentially a 
gap. 
 
3.4. Annual budgets, by definition, are short term in nature and so the MTFS provides 
financial forecasts over the medium term but the further out these forecasts project, 


=== PDF PAGE 9 ===
 
 
the greater the uncertainty with the financial estimates due to the number of factors 
which are beyond the Council’s direct control. 
 
3.5. This is particularly true in relation to government funding.  In December 2025, the 
Government published the first multi-year Local Government Finance Settlement 
since 2016/17. Following the 2016/17 settlement, which provided indicative funding 
through to 2019/20, subsequent settlements were issued on a one-year basis. This 
prolonged period of annual settlements significantly constrained the Council’s ability 
to undertake reliable medium-term financial planning. The new multi-year settlement, 
covering 2026/27 to 2028/29, has restored a degree of funding certainty and has 
enabled the Council to develop and present a five-year Medium Term Financial Plan 
(MTFP). For years 4 and 5 of the MTFP, which fall outside the published settlement 
period, projections have been based on prudent CPI-linked assumptions. 
 
3.6. Due to the inherent risk and uncertainty, the Council’s financial strategy should also 
include sensitivity analysis whereby the impact of changes to the key variables e.g. 
funding is assessed. This scenario testing allows the Council to make a judgement as 
to the most likely scenario to underpin its financial planning assumptions and assess 
the financial impact of different scenarios. 
 
3.7. It should be remembered that the Council has finite resources within which to deliver 
services and meet strategic objectives. Inevitably, decisions will need to strike a 
balance between the relevant service priorities, statutory obligations and funding 
envelope within which the Council must operate.  Whilst the Council exists for the 
benefit of providing services to residents, balancing the respective needs of those 
has always been challenging. 
 
3.8. Continuing to assess and consider how best to meet the needs of its service users is 
a continual process and understanding changing demographics is an important part 
of this assessment. Changes can impact not just on demand but also the way that 
users want to access or use Council services. 
 
3.9. For Barking & Dagenham, the demographics have changed significantly over the last 
decade, and this has had a particular impact on the nature of the demand for social 
care services alongside the significant national challenges regarding the cost of 
social care placements. The cost of children’s residential placements has increased 
substantially in the last five years according to local analysis of placements across 
this period. A greater proportion of the borough’s population is now much younger, 
although the Council has seen a significant increase in demand and complexity of 
support needs for its working-age population and this has led to significant financial 
pressures. 
 
3.10. However, as highlighted above, objectives over the medium term will need to be 
prioritised to ensure that service delivery is achieved through the approved financial 
framework. The key principles underpinning the Barking & Dagenham MTFS are set 
out below: 
 
a) A Council-wide approach to support addressing the drivers and solutions for 
social care demand. For example, how the Council’s Housing Strategy can 
better support older people and those with learning disabilities. Improvements 
to the digitalisation advice and guidance offer, so people are supported earlier 
and easier when they need help.

f) Comprehensive Asset Management Strategy is developed to ensure that the

independent stock condition surveys. 
 
g) Continue to monitor the local Council Tax Support Scheme (CTRS) – The level

h) Fees & Charges income – the Council will seek to ensure that it achieves full-

i) Evidence & Insight – having and using robust evidence and insight will be

6. Local Government Finance Settlement 
 
6.1. It is widely acknowledged that the funding of local authorities was in radical, urgent 
need of reform. As part of this, fair funding is also needed to reflect the borough’s 
significantly higher population and increased needs. Government has introduced 
major reforms to make funding fairer, simpler, and more reflective of local need. The 
new distribution model uses formulas to assess relative need and local revenue 
raising capacity.  
 
6.2. In recent years, Government has issued single year settlements, creating uncertainty 
for medium‑term planning. However, following the national funding reforms, the 
Provisional Local Government Finance Settlement (PLGFS) for 2026/27 provided, for 
the first time in several years, a three‑year indicative settlement covering the period 
2026/27 to 2028/29. 
 
6.3. The 2026/27 PLGFS was published on 17 December 2025 and the Final Settlement 
on 9 February 2026. Overall Core Spending Power (CSP) will increase by 5.7% 
across England. 
 
6.4. The PLGFS incorporates the results of the Funding Reform and the Fair Funding 
Review 2.0:   
 
6.4.1. Resetting the business rates retention system - The government has confirmed 
that the national business rates retention system will be reset from 2026/27, following 
the Fair Funding Review. This reset is intended to rebalance the system, ensuring 
funding more accurately reflects current levels of need while still recognising areas 
that have achieved genuine business rates growth. All business rates currently 
retained by local government will continue to be retained after the reset. However, 
distribution will shift to reflect new relative need shares, except where bespoke 
arrangements apply. New Baseline Funding Levels (BFLs) and Business Rates 
Baselines (BRBs) will together determine revised top-up and tariff positions for every 
authority from 2026/27. 
 
6.4.2. Distribution of Revenue Support Grant (RSG) - The government intends to use the 
RSG as the main mechanism for phasing in the new 2028/29 fair funding shares over 
the multi-year Settlement. RSG allocations will shift each year, by roughly one-third 
increments, to move authorities from their current funding position to their assessed 
fair funding level. This phasing takes account of the 2026/27 business rates reset and 
the continuation of the Better Care Grant, with full alignment achieved by 2028/29. 
This means the Council’s RSG will change annually as legacy funding is gradually 
removed. 
 
6.4.3. Consolidation of funding streams into the RSG – The government will consolidate 
18 separate grants into Revenue Support Grant from 2026/27, simplifying the system 
and phasing these resources into the new fair funding shares over the multi-year 
Settlement. This brings major social care, housing, and legacy grants into a single 
funding mechanism, while protecting a small number of specialist grants and keeping 
the Better Care Grant paid separately. 
 
6.5. The overall Core Spending power across London will increase by 5.6% and for 
Barking & Dagenham it will increase by 11.5%. While this uplift is welcomed and 
makes a meaningful contribution toward closing the 2026/27 budget gap, it does not

8. Business Rates 
 
8.1. From April 2026, the national Business Rates system for England will undergo its 
most significant change since the 2017 and 2023 revaluations. This includes a new 
five‑tier multiplier system, the end of temporary Retail, Hospitality and Leisure (RHL) 
relief, and the introduction of permanently lower RHL multipliers, alongside updated 
Rateable Values (RV) under the 2026 revaluation. 
 
8.2. RVs are determined based on the rental value of a property and reviewed every three 
years by the Valuation Office Agency. These values form the basis for calculating 
Business Rates. 
 
8.3. New Multiplier System 
 
8.3.1. The current two‑tier system (small business and standard multipliers) will be replaced 
by five multipliers, designed to reflect both property types and value. This change is 
designed to provide permanent support for RHL properties through lower multipliers 
and shifts more of the overall tax burden onto high-value hereditaments with RVs 
above £500k. 
 
8.3.2. In LBBD, although most businesses fall within the small-business category, around 
40% of the borough’s total 2026/27 rates liability will come from properties above 
£500k RV, meaning the new high-value multiplier has a significant local impact. 
 
8.3.3. The table below sets out the five multipliers applying nationally from 1 April 2026.

9. Business Rate Pool 
 
9.1. The business rates reset fundamentally changes the financial landscape for 
authorities participating in business rates pools. Once the reset takes effect, the 
growth generated locally will be removed from the system, meaning the financial 
advantage previously gained through pooling arrangements will no longer 
materialise. Given this shift, remaining in a pool is no longer financially beneficial or 
strategically justified. Therefore, the Council will not continue in a business rates pool 
from 2026/27 onwards. This position ensures that our financial planning reflects the 
new national framework and avoids the administrative burden of a pooling 
arrangement that no longer provides value.

P&R key movement since Quarter 2 (exception-based): 
 
16.1.4. The directorate is forecasting a £7.7m overspend at Quarter 3, compared with a 
breakeven position at Quarter 2 (including management actions) after applying £1.6m 
of reserves. This represents a 5.4% overspend against a budget of £143m. 
 
16.1.5. The overspend reflects previously reported service pressures, driven by a 
combination of increased demand, greater complexity of need, and cost pressures 
across both Adults’ and Children’s services. The Directorate continues to implement 
measures to reduce in-year expenditure and realign activity with the approved 
budget, while ensuring that services are maintained and the Council continues to 
meet all statutory duties. 
 
16.1.6. A corporately held contingency of £4m, earmarked specifically to address pressures 
in P&R has been recognised in Quarter 3 (and shows as a £4m underspend in 
Corporate Items) partially offset the pressure. 
 
16.1.7. The financial challenges facing P&R in the current financial year are expected to 
persist, and potentially intensify, over the coming period. As such, they are highly 
relevant to assessing the robustness of the 2026/27 budget. 
 
16.1.8. The key drivers remain as: 
 
• Adults Care & Support: A £4.9m pressure driven by increased demand, greater 
complexity of need, inflationary and market-driven uplifts, and high-cost Learning 
Disability placements, some reaching £5k per week. Pressures are further 
exacerbated by delayed hospital discharges and wider NHS system strain, which 
continue to increase demand on local care provision. 
• Children’s Care & Support: A £2.7m pressure resulting from significant placement 
cost inflation, including a 46% increase in private residential placement costs, 
higher placement volumes (+49 children in care compared with 2024), and the 
partial delivery of MTFS savings. The combination of rising unit costs and 
increased demand continues to impact the budget position. 
 
16.1.9. The associated risks remain broadly consistent with those reported at Quarter 2, 
although additional quantification is now available in several areas at Quarter 3, 
providing greater clarity on the scale of financial exposure. Key risks with quantified 
pressures are summarised below: 
 
• Homecare Demand Growth: Demand for Homecare continues to rise faster than 
anticipated despite assumptions of reductions through reablement, contributing to 
a forecast £3m overspend at Period 9. 
• Commissioning Savings Non-Delivery: £0.7m of commissioning savings will not 
be delivered, following a 50% reduction in NHS funding (ICBs and NHS 
England). In-year mitigations are being explored alongside a planned full review 
of the Commissioning Service. 
• Transitions and Higher-Cost Care Types: Increased movement between care 
types, particularly from Direct Payments to more expensive Supported Living/LD 
arrangements has created additional financial pressure, including a net weekly 
increase of £4.2k, compounded by a rise in the Learning Disability cohort due to 
new arrivals in the borough.

18. Financial Risks  
 
18.1. As highlighted throughout this report, the Council is facing material financial 
pressures which require careful management in setting its budget for 2026/27 and 
planning for the future to ensure that it is financial sustainable. 
 
18.2. Due to funding constraints, the Council will always have a finite level of financial 
resources both for operational service delivery and investment in those services or 
assets. However, demand for services can be unpredictable and, more importantly, 
difficult to control. 
 
18.3. The Council requires transformational change to operate within the proposed budget 
envelope and to ensure that it is financially sustainable over the medium to longer 
term.  
 
18.4. There are key inherent risks that most London local authorities face in setting their 
budgets although the level of risks for each can be very different: 
 
• Macro-economic factors - inflation, interest rates and supply chain problems 
• National issues – recruitment of skilled staff (shortages across most professions)  
• Demographic change: Barking & Dagenham has seen the fastest demographic 
change across the country 
• Demand and complexity of need - adults and children’s social care and SEND 
• Demand - increase in numbers of statutory homelessness duty 
• Funding not keeping pace with costs or demand 
• Additional statutory duties but without increased burdens funding 
• Insufficient funding for investment in services or assets 
• Assumptions and estimates underpinning the budgets prove to be inaccurate 
• Cost-of-living crisis impacting on citizens ability to pay their debts 
• Rising responsibilities and expectation levels for local authorities across most 
sectors, including social care and housing. 
 
18.5. The Council’s Section 151 Officer must make a statement to accompany the 
proposed budgets that confirms their opinion on whether the Council’s forecast 
reserves are adequate for the financial risks that the Council is facing. To assess this, 
effort should be made to quantify those risks.

on the management of demand or market challenges

19. Reserves 
 
19.1. The level of reserves is a key component of a robust and prudent medium-term 
financial strategy ensuring that funds are set aside for specific purposes or can be 
called upon to provide a buffer in the event of any unforeseen financial pressure. 
They are in effect the “shock absorbers” of the council’s finances and are the last line 
of defence to ensure resilience. 
 
19.2. Reserves can be classed as general reserve or earmarked reserves, and they 
represent funds that are not part of the normal recurring budget but are distinct “pots” 
of finite funds. Good practice would be to maintain General Reserves to an 
appropriate level in line with an approved Reserves Policy. 
 
19.3. The Cabinet was presented with a Reserve Policy in July 2023 which recommended 
that a General Reserve balance of £12m be maintained and it is proposed that this 
remains the case for next year. The budget proposals in this report support the 
maintenance of this balance.

£111.65m. Opening reserves were £130.43m.

23. Legal Implications  
 
Implications completed by: Dr Paul Feild, Principal Governance & Standards Solicitor 
 
23.1. Local authorities are under an explicit statutory duty to ensure that their financial 
management is adequate and effective and that they have a sound system of internal 
control and management of financial risk. This is set by sound public accounting 
practice guidance. 
 
23.2. The Local Government Act 2003 (2003 Act) Section 25 sets a specific duty on a local 
authority’s section 151 Local Government Act 1972, Chief Financial Officer (our 
Director of Finance and Investments), to make a budget calculation report to the said 
authority for it to take into account when it is considering its budget and funding for 
the forthcoming year. Their report must deal with the robustness of the estimates and 
the adequacy of the reserves included within the budget and the authority must have 
regard to the report in making its decisions. Section 26 of the 2003 Act gives the 
Secretary of State power to set a minimum level of reserves for which an authority 
must provide in setting its budget. The Secretary of State stated that ‘the provisions 
are a fall back against the circumstances in which an authority does not act 
prudently, disregards the advice of its Chief Financial Officer and is heading for 
serious financial difficulty’.  
 
23.3. By law a local authority is required under section 32 of the Local Government 
Finance Act 1992 to produce a ‘balanced budget’. The current budget setting takes 
place in the context of significant and widely known reductions in public funding to 
local authorities. Where there are reductions or changes in service provision as a 
result of changes in the financial position an authority is free to vary its policy and 
consequent service provision but at the same time must have regard to public law 
considerations in making any decision lawfully as any decision eventually taken is 

=== PDF PAGE 41 ===
 
 
also subject to judicial review. Members would also wish in any event to ensure 
adherence to proper processes as part of good governance.  
 
23.4. Part 1 of the 2003 Act requires a local authority body to each year set out its 
Treasury Management Strategy for borrowing and to prepare an Annual Investment 
Strategy which sets out its policies for managing its investments and for giving priority 
to the security and liquidity of those investments.  
 
23.5. A local authority also has to prepare an Annual Minimum Revenue Provision Policy 
Statement setting out how it proposes to repay its debts. The form of the policy must 
accord with prudential public finance accounting principles as espoused by the 
Chartered Institute of Public Finance Accountants (CIPFA). The relevant guidance is 
set out in the CIPFA Code of Practice on Treasury Management in the Public 
Services and the CIPFA Prudential Code for Capital Finance in Local Authorities 
(See the Treasury Management Strategy for the details).  
 
23.6. In terms of the budget and the effect it would have on service provision, whenever 
there are proposals for the closure or discontinuance of a service or services, there 
will be a need for appropriate consultation.  For example, if savings proposals will 
affect staffing it will require consultation with unions and staff. In relation to the impact 
on different groups, it should be noted that the Equality Act 2010 provides that a 
public authority must, in the exercise of its functions, have due regard to the need to 
eliminate discrimination and to advance equality of opportunity between persons who 
do and those who do not share a relevant ‘protected characteristic’. This means an 
assessment needs to be carried out of the impact and a decision taken in the light of 
such information. In addition to that, Members will need to be satisfied that Equality 
Impact Assessments have been carried out before the proposals are decided by 
Cabinet.  
 
23.7. If at any point resort to constricting expenditure is required, it is important that due 
regard is given to statutory duties and responsibilities including the duty to make 
services better. Section 3 of the Local Government Act 1999 requires that local 
authorities must make arrangements to secure continuous improvement in the way in 
which its functions are exercised, having regard to a combination of economy, 
efficiency and effectiveness. This duty to make improvement also requires (section 
3(2) of the 1999 Act), that in doing so consultation must be carried out.  
 
23.8. Additional considerations the Council must have regard to in taking decisions are: 
 
• any existing contractual obligations covering current service provision. Such 
contractual obligations where they exist must be fulfilled or varied with agreement 
of current providers;  
• any legitimate expectations that persons already receiving a service may have to 
either continue to receive the service or to be consulted directly before the 
service is withdrawn;  
• any rights which statute may have conferred on individuals and as a result of 
which the council may be bound to continue its provision. This could be where an 
assessment has been carried out for example for special educational needs 
statement of special educational needs in the education context);  
• the impact on different groups affected by any changes to service provision as 
informed by relevant equality impact assessments.

24. Other Implications 
 
24.1. Risk Management - The Council recognises that maintaining a dynamic risk aware 
culture is vitally important as it goes through periods of change, with the increasing 
need to balance the effects of budget reductions, changes to services provided and 
possible increased demand. The benefits gained in managing risk effectively are 
improved strategic, operational and financial management, better decisions and 
outcome delivery, improved statutory compliance and ultimately improving the 
services that people receive.  
 
The significant risks have been identified in this report, with impacts from those risks 
highlighted for consideration where possible. Controls and actions to manage risk are 
included within this report and have been integrated into the implementation plan to 
deliver the Budget Strategy. The Strategic Risk Register identifies ‘Financial 
Management’ as a key risk to achieving priorities, and this report contributes to the 
mitigation therein. 
 
24.2. Contractual Issues - The expectation is that all procurements conducted will follow 
the processes as set out in the Council’s Contract Rules, the Procurement Act 2023, 
which came into force in February 2025, and the Public Contracts Regulations 2015 
(for procurements started before the Procurement Act 2023 came into force). All high 
risk/value procurements will be supported by Corporate Procurement to ensure that 
the process drives the commercial outcomes needed by the Council whilst delivering 
the service requirements. 
 
24.3. Staffing Issues – The Council has agreed organisational change procedures which 
comply with legislation and have been agreed with Trade Unions. Formal and 
meaningful consultation with staff and trade unions take place when proposals to 
review a service are made. The Council is under a legal duty to issue an HR1 and 
S188 notice when considering redundancies including deletion of vacant posts. This 
has been undertaken by the Chief Executive and will be kept under review. Any 
redundancies are made in line with the Council’s redundancy policy and 
redeployment opportunities are fully explored.  
 
In addition to specific consultation with staff and Trade Unions on restructure 
proposals, under the Council’s policy, a number of all staff engagement sessions 
have been held run by the Chief Executive and other members of the Executive 
Team. 
 
24.4. Corporate Policy and Equality Impact - When setting this budget, the Council has 
been mindful of the need to protect and support vulnerable people. The Census, Joint 
Strategic Needs Assessment, and other key data sets highlight the levels of need, 
deprivation, inequality and disadvantage faced by our population. The other driver of 
approach to budget setting is to ensure delivery of the Corporate Plan priorities, and, 
more fundamentally, that the Council’s resources are allocated and deployed in a 
way that guarantees statutory obligations will be met. 
 

=== PDF PAGE 43 ===
 
 
As part of the Public Sector Equality Duty, under the Equality Act 2010, the Council 
must ensure due regard to eliminating discrimination, advancing equality of 
opportunity and fostering good relations between people who share a protected 
characteristic and those who do not. This applies for day-to-day operations and in 
decision-making about policy and service changes.   As part of the process of 
developing savings proposals Equality Impact Assessments were carried out using a 
consistent framework and method of assessment. As a minimum standard, all 
savings proposals were screened for equalities and service user impacts.   Review of 
Equality Impact Assessment screening tools concludes the new savings proposals 
have a neutral impact for people with protected characteristics, or there is no material 
impact due to the proposal not changing the services provided, including how they 
are accessed or experienced by the service user. Similarly, it was determined the 
proposals would not affect the outcomes of the service user.  Due to these impacts 
the cumulative impact assessment of the MTFS 2026/27 (see Appendix J) is that 
there is neutral impact.  
 
The conclusion is the Council’s proposals for achieving savings are considered 
reasonable and have shown due regard to the Public Sector Equality Duty.
