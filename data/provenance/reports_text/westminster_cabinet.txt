1. Executive Summary 
1.1. This report brings together the Council’s business and financial planning and 
looks forward over the next three years to set out how it will support the 
Council’s objectives under the Fairer Westminster strategy, supported by a 
medium-term financial plan and balanced budget for 2026/27. Cabinet is 
asked to consider the report and recommend its adoption to Full Council on 4 
March 2026. 
1.2. This report outlines a balanced budget for 2026/27 that includes a proposed 
2% increase in council tax and a one-off use of reserves of £19.7m. The 
medium-term financial plan forecasts an ongoing budget gap of £87.8m by the 
third year. Central government funding for 2026/27 is confirmed and indicative 
grant figures are provided through the multi-year settlement for 2027/28 and 
2028/29.  
1.3. The proposed 2026/27 revenue budget assumes a freeze in the general 
council tax element and a 2% increase for the adult’s social care precept 
element. At Band D this will result in an annual increase of £10.54 or an 
equivalent weekly amount of 20p per week. When combined, the general 
element of council tax and adults social care precept, for Westminster, will 
rise from £526.80 to £537.34 at Band D. 
1.4. The proposed budget reflects the significant reduction in grant funding that the 
Council will be impacted by through the changes that have been made as part 
of the Fair Funding Review.  The reductions are phased over the next three 
years, with £75.6m of annual funding coming out by the third year.  Floor 
protection funding of £24.9m remains in the third year.  
1.5. In light of the major changes to grant funding the Council has scoped out a 
whole council transformation programme which will ensure the organisation 
can maintain services and deliver them while remaining modern, people-
centred and financially resilient. This programme requires investment in the 
short term but can deliver substantial efficiencies and a minimum forecast of 
£30m is included. 
1.6. The recommended net General Fund budget for 2026/27 is £282.666m, this is 
a net decrease of £11.4m on the 2025/26 adjusted net budget.  The budget 
changes include costs pressures and investments totalling £56.4m including; 
further Fairer Westminster investments of £2.1m, additional resources for 
temporary accommodation and Adults Social Care pressures of £9.3m, other 
service cost pressures including inflation of £27.6m, increased capital 
financing costs of £3.5m, decreased interest earnings of £6m, transformation 
investment of £2.5m and other changes of £5.3m.  
1.7. Savings and cost reductions totalling £54.5m including new savings of 
£27.8m, transformation savings of £3m, previously agreed savings proposals 
of £12.7m, reduced employer pension contributions of £8m and council tax 
collection fund surplus of £3m. Fair Funding grant reductions including HPG 
grant loss totalling £26.4m. The balance is then funded from increased council

5. Funding Reform 
Background 
5.1. The Government’s Fair Funding Review was first announced in December 
2017 to modernise and update the way local government funding is allocated 
across England. The existing system, based on outdated formulas and data, 
was widely regarded as complex and opaque. The review aimed to create a 
simpler, more transparent approach that better reflected local authorities’ 
relative needs and resources, while supporting financial sustainability and 
multi-year settlements.  
5.2. Initial consultations in 2018 focused on developing new formulas for 
assessing need and resource capacity, alongside proposals to align the 
review with the planned expansion of business rates retention. However, 
implementation was delayed, and the original proposals were never fully 
realised. At that time, it was understood that Westminster was likely to be 
adversely impacted by the proposed changes due to its high taxbase and 
reduced population. Consequently, an estimated reduction in funding of £27m 
over three years was built into the Medium-Term Financial Plan (MTFP) 
reflecting an estimated maximum reduction in funding of £9m per annum.  
Fair Funding Review 
5.3. In 2025, the Government relaunched the Fair Funding Review with a renewed 
commitment to reform and a challenging timetable, with the new 
arrangements to be implemented from April 2026 onwards. The updated 
proposals sought to consolidate multiple funding streams into a single, 
streamlined system, update formulas used to measure relative need and more 
fully reflect local tax-raising capacity.  
5.4. In June 2025 Government issued a consultation setting out planned changes. 
Westminster raised significant concerns around the lack of recognition of the 
cost impacts of daytime visitors and the substantial housing cost pressures 
faced by residents which contribute to higher levels of deprivation. 
5.5. A policy statement was issued by the Government on the 20 November, 
alongside a comprehensive response to the summer consultation. Key 
elements included: 
 Continuation of the Recovery Grant for eligible authorities in 2026/27; 

=== PDF PAGE 7 ===
 
 
 
 
 Reaffirmation of the Government’s objective to delivery higher funding to 
authorities with the highest levels of deprivation; 
 Publication of updated Relative Needs Formulas including population 
projections and the Index of Multiple Deprivation including housing costs; 
 Reduced weighting for daytime population and no account taken of 
tourists; and 
 Introduction of new damping floors for authorities losing funding under the 
reforms. 
5.6. The provisional Local Government Finance Settlement for 2026/27 to 2028/29 
was published on 17 December 2025. It outlined provisional funding allocations 
for local authorities across the three years.  
 
5.7. The proposed changes have a significant adverse impact on Westminster. This 
is largely due to the implementation of updated population data, deprivation 
measures, reduced recognition for daytime visitor and full resource (council tax) 
equalisation. Despite transitional protections, the phased introduction of the 
new arrangements from 2026/27 to 2028/29 will create a substantial financial 
pressure for the Council. The revised formulas place greater emphasis on 
factors such as local tax-raising capacity and area cost adjustments, which do 
not fully capture the complexity of Westminster’s demographic pressures and 
service demands. As a result, the Council’s funding allocation under the new 
system does not adequately reflect the level of need. While the Council 
supports the principle of a fairer funding system, the proposed approach does 
not take account of the factors that drive significant costs in Westminster.   
 
National Picture

 Recovery grant (which Westminster does not receive); and 
 
 Four new consolidated grants.

Council tax 
 
5.20. Increases in CSP assume that income from council tax increases over the three 
years due to taxbase growth in line with the average annual growth in taxbase 
between 2022/23 and 2025/26 (equivalent to 1.4% for WCC) and that councils 
increase council tax by the maximum possible allowable under council tax 
referendum principles. The core referendum limit remains at up to 3 per cent for 
London Boroughs with the ability to set an additional 2 per cent adult social

Authority (GLA) will have a referendum principle of £5.13.  
 
5.21. For the six authorities noted earlier including Westminster, there will be no 
referendum limits for 2027/28 and 2028/29 if their Band D council tax levels are 
lower than average and they have 95% of their income protected. The 
Government have assumed in their CSP calculations that these authorities will 
make use of these flexibilities.  
 
Business Rates Retention and Reset  
 
5.22. A package of changes to business rates retention will be implemented in 
2026/27. The business rates retention system will be reset, properties have 
been revalued and new multipliers will be applied. However, in overall terms, 
there should be no bottom-line impact on authorities in the short term. In 
2026/27 the safety net threshold will be increased to 100% of the baseline 
funding level (i.e. the amount of business rates retained within authorities). This 
will then reduce to 97% in 2027/28 and revert to its current level of 92.5% in 
2028/29 onwards.   
 
5.23. The government will estimate how much each local authority will collect from 
April 2026 and how much they will retain according to their calculated relative 
needs and resources. Authorities will be able to keep any business rates 
growth that they have experienced since the start of the retention arrangements 
(2013/14).

6. Sector Wide Cost Pressures and Issues  
Service Pressures 
6.1. Nationally local authorities continue to face significant financial pressures 
driven primarily by rising demand, increasing complexity in service delivery and 
sustained inflationary pressures. Adults and Children’s social care remains the 
most significant area of pressure with increasing demand demographic 
changes, longer life expectancy, and more complex health and care needs 
placing sustained demand on services. These pressures are compounded by 
workforce shortages and escalating provider fees, which make it harder to 
maintain quality and capacity. 
 
6.2. Alongside social care, housing and homelessness costs have continued to 
increase, particularly in areas with acute affordability issues, while temporary 
accommodation demand continues to rise. At the same time, authorities are 
grappling with the impact of high inflation on core services, utilities, and 
contractual obligations. Together, these factors create a highly challenging 
environment for local authorities, requiring difficult decisions to balance budgets 
while meeting statutory obligations. 
 
Wider Economic Impacts 
6.3. In addition, the economic environment in which this budget is set remains 
highly challenging. Inflation, while easing from its peak, remains above historic 
norms, driving up costs across goods, services, and contractual obligations. 
The Office of Budget Responsibility (OBR) now expects inflation won't return 
the Bank of England 2% target until late 2026 / early 2027, one year later than 
previously forecast last March. At the same time, elevated interest rates 
increase the cost of local authority borrowing over the period of their capital 
programmes impacting future investment plans.  
 
6.4. Economic uncertainty and slower growth have contributed to rising demand for 
local services, as households face ongoing cost of living pressures. This has 
led to greater reliance on housing support, welfare services, and social care, 
adding complexity and financial strain to local authority budget planning. 
Against this backdrop, councils must continue to adopt a prudent and resilient 
approach to ensure financial stability while protecting essential services for 
residents.

redesign, transformation programmes, and long-term investment in technology

and careful management to avoid adverse impacts on service quality. As a 
result, delivering the next phase of savings will be more challenging and carries 
greater risk, as financial pressures continue to intensify.

12. Financial Resilience  
12.1. The UK’s economic outlook remains uncertain over the medium-term with 
several risks and uncertainties over inflation, interest rates and economic 
growth.  Whilst local authorities have more certainty over government funding 
through the provision of a three-year indicative multiyear finance settlement this 
forms only a small part of the Council’s financial landscape and councils face 
significant demand pressures for key services such as temporary 
accommodation and adults social care, whilst implementing council wide 
savings initiatives to balance the budget.  
12.2. The Council will continue to use robust and regular budget and performance 
monitoring to identify emerging pressures and the financial impact on budgets. 
Retaining reserves is a key mechanism to help mitigate against those risks 
impacting on the Council’s front-line services. The use of reserves in the 
2026/27 budget is considered justified as an exception, because of the late 

=== PDF PAGE 24 ===
 
 
 
 
impact of the funding review and the fact that longer term actions to stabilise 
the budget will not have had the time to make a significant impact.

Reserves Policy 
12.6. A key financial tool for the Council to manage the risks set out above is 
reserves - an important part of financial planning. They are held for two 
purposes: to mitigate risks or to invest in the Council’s priorities. 
12.7. Local authorities hold two categories of reserves, usable and unusable: 
 Usable reserves are defined as those that the Council could utilise to 
fund capital or revenue expenditure. Some of these reserves could be 
applied generally but others will have stipulations attached on their use, 
and 
 Unusable reserves hold unrealised gains or losses for assets not yet 
disposed of and accounting adjustments, which are required by statute. 
These reserves cannot be used to fund capital or revenue expenditure.  
12.8. The Council’s usable reserves can be grouped into the following sub-
categories:  
 General Reserves – working balances held to ensure long term 
solvency and to mitigate risks e.g. the General Fund balance and the 
Housing Revenue Account balance 
 Earmarked Reserves – to fund specific projects or investments, or to 
build up funds for known risks or contingencies, e.g. the capital 
financing reserve or the insurance reserve 
 Ring-fenced Reserves – carried forward balances or grant funding 
which have certain conditions or restrictions attached to them 
preventing their general use by the Council, e.g. schools’ balances, 
public health grant or homeless prevention grant 
 Capital Reserves – amounts held to finance capital expenditure, e.g. 
receipts from asset disposals and capital grants.  

=== PDF PAGE 28 ===

Westminster’s reserves in a National Context 
12.15.  Evidence from the National Audit Office (NAO) shows there has been 
pressure within the sector on the use of reserves to balance budgets. The 
mismatch between demand pressures and funding has led to reserves reducing 
at an accelerated rate and has been a contributing factor to 42 Councils 
requiring Exception Financial Support (EFS) in 2024/25.  
12.16. CIPFA’s most recent guidance on reserves emphasises that: 

=== PDF PAGE 30 ===
 
 
 
 
 reserves should not be used to fund ongoing expenditure except as part of a 
time limited, credible medium-term strategy.  
 Councils must maintain sufficient reserves to absorb major financial shocks, 
including demand spikes, funding volatility and slippage in savings delivery. 
 the Section 151 Officer must advise on the adequacy of reserves annually, 
in line with statutory requirements under the Local Government Act 2003. 
12.17. Westminster’s strategy of using reserves to manage multi-year pressures 
while maintaining high reserve levels relative to its Core Spending Power is 
consistent with these principles. 
 
12.18. While Westminster’s reserve levels remain high compared with other 
authorities (partly due to substantial capital and business rates risk reserves), 
the expected level of depletion over the next three years highlights several 
sustainability risks if reserves were to be used for day-to-day expenditure.  
 
12.19. The use of earmarked and general reserves can support short and medium-
term stability but cannot form a permanent solution to structural funding gaps. 
This principle is strongly reinforced by both CIPFA and the National Audit 
Office.  
 
12.20. Westminster’s reserve strategy remains prudent because: 
 the Council plans to maintain high levels of usable reserves, at a 
significantly higher proportion of Core Spending Power than the national 
average 
 reserve uses are aligned with managing demand risks, absorbing inflation, 
and smoothing expenditure linked to the capital programme. 
12.21. The position the Council finds itself in, as one of the authorities with the 
biggest reduction in grant funding across the country means that the ability to 
use reserves over the medium term will be crucial to being able to introduce 
significant change to reduce net spending in a carefully planned way. It is clear 
that one-off use of reserves will not be able to solve the financial challenge 
faced by the Council going forward.

Council Tax Reduction Scheme  
13.15. The Local Government Finance Act 2012 replaced the previous national 
Council Tax Benefit scheme with a locally determined Council Tax Reduction 
Scheme (also known as a local Council Tax Support (CTS) scheme) from 2013.  

=== PDF PAGE 34 ===
 
 
 
 
13.16. Each local authority is required to annually set a local Council Tax Reduction 
scheme for working age claimants. The government continues to operate a 
statutory national scheme for pensioners, which provides them with broadly the 
same level of Council Tax Support as they received under the previous Council 
Tax Benefit scheme, but which has been adjusted by the government since its 
introduction to incorporate several welfare reform initiatives. 
13.17. Since 2013/14, the Council has agreed a Council Tax Support scheme which 
mirrored the previous Council Tax Benefit scheme (i.e. a 100% scheme) 
despite resources from government being reduced by 10% at the time of 
transfer. This ensured Westminster’s working age claimants didn’t have to pay 
more Council Tax. Technically this means that the original Council Tax 
Reduction Schemes (Default Scheme) Regulations are mirrored within the City 
Council’s local scheme, with the addition that rates used to calculate the 
discount are uprated each year, and War Disabled Pensions, War Widow, 
Pensions and Armed Forces Compensation scheme payments are disregarded 
in full when calculating a claimant’s income. 
13.18. The Council recently approved the retention of the “100%” Council Tax 
Reduction scheme for the 2026/27 financial year. Whilst many other local 
authorities have reduced the level of their Council Tax Support (which means 
their CTS claimants must contribute more to Council tax), Westminster’s 
decision will protect claimants locally. 
Second Home Premium 
13.19. The Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023 became law on 26 October 
2023. The legislation allows for a Second Home Premium (additional charge) 
on second homes of up to 100%. The ability to charge a Second Home 
Premium technically came into effect on 1 April 2024, although there was a 
legislative requirement to delay implementation for 12 months before the 
premium could be implemented. Central government advised that this delay 
was to “give property owners a chance to respond, for example by selling or 
letting their properties”.  
13.20. In December 2025 the Cabinet agreed to continue to implement a 100% 
Second Home Premium for the 2026/27 financial year. It is estimated that this 
premium will result in c.£6m of additional income for the Council. 
The Collection Fund 
13.21. Statutory regulations require local authorities to account for annual council 
tax/ business rates income in a different way to normal accounting 
arrangements as would apply if using International Financial Reporting 
Standards (IFRS). This means any variance between the originally estimated 
net council tax / business rates yield and what is achieved in year is not 
immediately recognised and is held on the balance sheet to be distributed in 
subsequent years. The effect of these regulations is that for 2026/27 the above 
estimates will represent the amount of income credited to the revenue account 
for that year regardless of the actual achieved.

13.22. The Council has been reporting a deficit in its Collection Fund business 
rates account since 2020/21, except for a one-off surplus generated in 2023/24 
due to the release of funds previously held to finance the cost of business rate 
appeals on the 2017 rating list. The Council is currently forecasting to continue 
to be at a below baseline position for 2025/26, in which it will lose 7.5% of its

Business Rates 
13.25. Significant change is being implemented all at the same time from April 2026 
in the national system for business rates, which is also aligning with revenue 
support grants for Councils through the Fair Funding Review. 
 
13.26.  A national revaluation of business rates will be applied from 1 April 2026, in 
which rateable values have been updated by the Valuation Office Agency to 
reflect the market value of non-domestic properties as of 1 April 2024. The total 
rateable value for businesses in Westminster has increased by 22.3%, which is 
marginally above the national average increase of 19.4%. As a result of the 
overall increase in the total rateable value for England, Central Government 
have reduced the value of the standard multiplier to 48.0p in 2026/27 (55.5p in 
2025/26), and the small multiplier to 43.2p (49.9p in 2025/26). The increase in 
total rateable value in Westminster due to the revaluation is therefore largely 
offset by the corresponding reduction in the national multipliers.  
 
13.27. The government has also introduced permanently lower standard and small 
multipliers for retail, hospitality and leisure (RHL) properties with a rateable 
value below £500,000 from 2026/27, which follows the removal of the 
temporary RHL relief that has been provided since the pandemic. This will be 
funded by the introduction of a new high-value multiplier for non-domestic 

=== PDF PAGE 36 ===
 
 
 
 
properties with a rateable value of over £500,000. There will be no loss or 
additional income retained by the Council due to these new multipliers, as the 
S31 grant system will be used by central government to compensate authorities 
for lost revenue due to RHL discounts, and to collect additional revenue due to 
the high-value multiplier supplement.  
 
13.28. The impact of the business rates revaluation and changes to the multipliers is 
an increase in Westminster’s gross debit, i.e. the total amount of business rates 
that must be paid by entities within the City, from £2.602bn to £2.735bn.  This is 
an overall increase of 5.1%.  
 
13.29. The government will also implement a full reset of the Business Rates 
Retention System from 2026/27, as business rate baselines, baseline funding 
levels and resulting top-up/tariff amounts for each local authority are to be reset 
as part of the wider Fair Funding Review. The value of the new business rates 
baseline and tariff payable for the Council has been determined using the draft 
2026 valuation list published in November 2025 and will be adjusted to reflect 
the final valuation list as of 1 April 2026 during the 2026/27 financial year.  
Given that the Council is forecasting to be below its baseline position in 
2026/27 and that the level of safety net protection is increasing to 100% for the 
first year of the multi-year settlement, any additional tariff payable due to this 
revaluation adjustment will be covered by additional safety net receipts from 
central government. 
Business Rates: The Collection Fund and Pooling 
13.30. The Council was part of the 2018/19, 2019/20 and 2020/21 London Business

for 2021/22 due to the volatility in business rates following the pandemic and 
expected reduction in business rates income. Therefore, Councils returned to 
the previous business rates share regime that allocated income based on the 
following proportions: WCC 30%, GLA 37% and central government 33%. In 
recent years a smaller group of boroughs agreed to pool but will not continue to 
do so in 2026/27, as it is no longer viable following the redistribution of 
accumulated growth within the reset of the business rates retention system.

High Value Council Tax Surcharge  
13.33. In the Chancellor’s budget in November 2025, it was announced that a high 
value council tax surcharge would be implemented from April 2028. This will be 
an annual tax, collected alongside and in addition to the council tax on 
properties valued at £2 million or more. The charge will be a flat-rate ranging 
from £2,500 to £7,500. The new tax is expected to affect less than 1% of 
properties nationally, however that percentage will be significantly higher in 
Westminster. The additional money collected will be paid to the Treasury.

14.1. As part of the budget setting process each year there is also a statutory

Capital Strategy 
14.2. The Capital Strategy sets out the Council’s long term capital investment plans

14.3. The Council’s long-term capital investment is underpinned by the objectives of

the capital financing budget to £3.5m each additional year. The affordability of

Housing Revenue Account Business Plan 
14.5. The Housing Revenue Account will be budgeting a breakeven position in 
2026/27 
14.6. The HRA capital programme is the vehicle through which the Council identifies 
and prioritises funding to support two important programmes of investment: 
 a Planned Maintenance Programme that seeks to improve the condition of 
existing HRA stock; and 

=== PDF PAGE 38 ===
 
 
 
 
 a Development Programmes which seeks to deliver new affordable 
housing. 
14.7. As far as borrowing to fund the capital programme is concerned, the HRA is 
subject to the CIPFA Prudential Code and must therefore set borrowing limits 
that are sustainable within the context of the 30-year business plan. This 
means that all borrowing is ultimately limited to the extent to which the 30-year 
HRA Business Plan can afford the service the interest costs on new debt. 
14.8. The current approved HRA business plan sets two simple parameters to 
assess the sustainable borrowing capacity and viability of the HRA: 
 a minimum HRA reserve level of 10% of turnover / 15% of rents; and 
 a minimum interest cover ratio of 1.20 to ensure that the operating surplus 
is not entirely committed to covering interest costs. This also ensures that 
the business plan can tolerate some volatility on interest rates or inflation. 
14.9. The MTFP process has involved a complete review and refresh of all 
assumptions that go into the business plan. This focussed on setting budgets 
that meet the specific objectives and priorities of the service, with a continued 
strategic commitment to maximise HRA borrowing capacity to support the 
delivery of new social and affordable housing. This is set alongside the need to 
mitigate against the impact of inflation on costs. The outcome of this means 
that the HRA will need to deliver ongoing savings in the medium term to 
maintain a balanced 30-Year Business Plan. 
Rent Setting 
14.10. As social rents are the main source of income for the HRA Business Plan, a 
key consideration for the refresh is future rent levels. Rent increases are 
subject to the Rent Standard, which stipulates annual rent increases of CPI+1% 
(based on September CPI at 3.8%). However, in addition Government have 
recently announced a Social Rent Convergence policy that proposes an 
additional £1 per week in 2027/28 and £2 per week from 2028/29, which would 
be in addition to the Rent Standard increase. This has been built into the 
business plan for a 10-year period from 2027/28. 
Risk Measures 
14.11. Whilst national economic factors can create some financial challenges for the 
HRA business plan, each iteration of the plan contains a series of measures 
designed to help the HRA to manage such volatility. The capital programme 
continues to hold a contingency, the revenue budget has a set level of interest 
cover, and the HRA reserves exist to manage short-term shocks. These are all 
elements of the business plan that can be leveraged to keep the HRA on a 
robust and sustainable financial footing, and a thorough review of appropriate 
risk cover will form another integral part of the business plan refresh.

14.12. The annual Treasury Management Strategy Statement (TMSS) is presented

Chancellor’s Autumn Budget will also create expectations of further rate cuts.

17. Legal Implications 
17.1. The function of calculating the City Council’s budget requirement and the City 
Council’s element of the Council Tax, and the function of setting the Council 
Tax, are the responsibility of the full Council. The function of preparing 
estimates and calculations for submission to the full Council is the responsibility 
of the Cabinet.  
17.2. In coming to decisions in relation to the revenue budget (and the Council Tax), 
the Council and its officers have various statutory duties. In general terms, the 
Council is required by the Local Government Finance Act 1992 to make 
estimates of gross revenue expenditure and anticipated income, leading to a 
calculation of a budget requirement and the setting of an overall budget (and 
Council Tax). The amount of the budget requirement must be sufficient to meet 
the City Council’s legal and financial obligations, ensure the proper discharge of 
its statutory duties, and lead to a balanced budget.  
17.3. The Council should be satisfied that the proposals put forward are a reasonably 
prudent use of resources in both the short and long term, and that the interests 
of both council taxpayers and ratepayers on the one hand and the users of 
Council services on the other are both considered.  
17.4. Section 25 of the Local Government Act 2003 requires that when a local 
authority is making its budget calculations, the Chief Finance Officer of the 
authority must report to the Council on the robustness of the estimates made 
for the purposes of the calculations and the adequacy of the proposed financial 
reserves. The Council has a statutory duty to have regard to the report of the 
Section 151 Officer on these issues when making decisions about its budget 
calculations. Attention is drawn to the report as set out in Section 10 where it 
is stated that the estimates are sufficiently robust for the purposes of the 
calculations and that the proposed financial balances and reserves over the 
medium term are adequate.  
17.5. Some savings proposals may only be delivered after specific statutory or other 
legal procedures have been followed and/or consultation taken place. Where 
consultation is required, the Council cannot rule out the possibility that they 
may change their minds on the proposal because of the responses to a 
consultation, and further reports to Cabinet or Cabinet Member 
(as appropriate) may be required.  

=== PDF PAGE 42 ===
 
 
 
 
17.6. Apart from statutory duties relating to specific proposals the Council must 
consider its obligations under the Equality Act. This is addressed in Section 16. 
In developing a final set of proposals for consideration, officers have had regard 
to how the equality duty can be fulfilled in relation to the proposals overall. 
However further detailed equality impact assessments may be required for 
specific proposals as identified by each directorate prior to final decisions being 
made.  
17.7. Section 106, Local Government Finance Act 1992, applies to Members where:  
 they are present at a meeting of the Council, the Cabinet or a Committee 
and at the time of the meeting an amount of Council Tax is payable by 
them and has remained unpaid for at least two months; and  
 any budget or Council Tax calculation, or recommendation or decision 
which might affect the making of any such calculation, is the subject of 
consideration at the meeting.   
17.8. In these circumstances, any such Members shall at the meeting and as soon 
as practicable after its commencement disclose the fact that Section 106 
applies to them and shall not vote on any question concerning the matter. Such 
Members are not debarred from speaking. Failure to comply with these 
requirements constitutes a criminal offence, unless any such members can 
prove they did not know that Section 106 applied to them at the time of the 
meeting or that the matter in question was the subject of consideration at the 
meeting.  
17.9. In relation to the use of General Fund and HRA (non-right to buy) capital 
receipts funds to fund transformation projects detailed in this report, the 
Council complies with the statutory guidance issued under section 15(1)(a) of 
the Local Government Act 2003.  
17.10. Under powers contained in the Localism Act 2011, the Government 
can require compulsory referendums on Council Tax increases above limits it 
sets. For 2025/6, the referendum threshold is 2.99%. The proposal is within the 
threshold change: the Council will therefore not be required to hold a 
referendum.  
17.11. In addition to the referendum threshold, the Government has also announced 
a threshold of an additional 2% for authorities with Social Care responsibilities. 
The borough needs to raise funding on this account for 2025/26 and is

19. Equalities Impact Assessment 
19.1. Under the Equalities Act 2010 the Council has a legal duty to pay “due regard” 
to the need to eliminate discrimination and promote equality about the 
protected characteristics of age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage/ 
civil partnership, pregnancy/ maternity, race, religion or belief and sexual 
orientation. 
19.2. The equality duties do not prevent the Council from making difficult decisions 
such as reorganisations and relocations, redundancies, and service 
reductions nor do they stop the Council from making decisions which may 
affect one group more than another. The law requires that the duty to pay 
“due regard” be demonstrated in the decision-making process. 
19.3. A screening of all budget measures has been undertaken to ensure that the 
equality duty has been considered where appropriate. Details of the Equality 
Impact Assessments (EIAs) are included in Appendix 7. Where it has been 
identified that a proposal may have an adverse impact on people who share a 
protected characteristic, an assessment of the impact has been undertaken to 
ensure that “due regard” is paid to the equality duties as required by statute. 
Where budget proposals required a full EIA to be undertaken, these have 
been published and shared with the Budget & Performance Task Group to 
ensure they form part of the budget scrutiny process. 
19.4. The legal implications for all savings proposals will be considered when 
approval is sought for each proposal. Each proposal will be approved in 
accordance with the council’s constitution.
