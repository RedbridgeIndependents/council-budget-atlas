REPORT SUMMARY 
 
Lambeth Council is on a path to financial sustainability . We have taken a number of significant steps to 
improve our financial resilience against a backdrop of continued funding pressures for local authorities and 
the effects over a number of years of major budget pressures in demand-led services. The increase to 
local government funding and a return to multi -year settlements by this Government is welcomed and 
much needed but it will take years to make up for the real terms reduction in funding experienced for well 
over a decade. The national housing crisis persists, reflected in the significant pressure on the Council’s 
resources, with spend on temporary accommodation expected to reach nearly £105m this year. This is a 
pressing issue across London as boroughs spend £5m a day  collectively on housing homeless 
households. 
 
Lambeth Council will keep making our case to Government to recognise the challenges we face as a result 
of national funding reforms.  It will not be easy to balance the resources needed to discharge our statutory 
duties and target support to our vulnerable residents who rely on our services the most. But the council is 
committed to transforming its services and ways of working to reduce costs and improve services and 
outcomes for Lambeth residents. 
 
It is a source of great pride that not only is Lambeth maintaining vital services in the face of significant 
demand and costs but we are also seeing significant improvement to the delivery of our services. In line 
with the ambitions set out in our borough plan Lambeth 2030, we will: 
• continue to prioritise good quality services for residents. building on the strong improvement 
records that have been highlighted in inspections in recent years by Ofsted, the C2 grading by the 
Regulator for Social Housing and our Youth Justice Service moving from ‘inadequate’ in 2015  to 
‘Good’ with outstanding features in this year’s inspection by His Majesty’s Inspectorate of 
Probation. 
• continue to support residents through the cost -of-living crisis, using the council’s investment and 
the Government’s new Crisis and Resilience Fund, building on our record of support to reduce the 
impact of poverty in the borough,  helping residents maximise their benefits and other targeted 
financial support and guidance. 
• continue to prioritise the resources we have on making the most impact for residents and 
businesses in line with their priorities, continuing to invest in making our neighbourhoods cleaner

These priorities, alongside ensuring our communities feel safe from anti -social behaviour and violence,

replenishing our reserves.

2026-27, which is assumed by Government in their Core Spending Power calculations.

Support scheme will still provide more than £27 million of support to tens of thousands of the lowest paid 
households, and maintain protections for pensioners, in addition to  the council’s Tackling Poverty 
programme which will provide over £5 million of support to vulnerable residents. Many of our residents will 
also receive more support through the Government’s lifting of the two-child benefit cap from April 2026.

We have already seen the positive impact on our financial position of decisions made in this current

• identifying options for new ways of working to be more efficient; 
• transforming the shape of council services over the coming years to ensure value for money and 
good quality services for our residents; 
• focusing on delivering savings and income generating proposals; 
• limiting the use of and replenishing reserves.

As with many authorities the council has applied for Exceptional Financial Support ( EFS) to support the 
General Fund. London Councils anticipate that as many as 17 London Councils will require EFS by 2028. 
Some authorities are finding themselves in the position that they are needing to use EFS to a substantial 
level on a year-by-year basis, but the council’s application is a short term measure in addition to the £40m 
of support for the Housing Revenue Account (HRA) in 2025- 26. EFS is not a grant . It allows councils to 
borrow or use capital receipts to fund day to day services helping them to maintain essential operations 
while working towards a more sustainable financial position. Reference to the EFS application is made 
throughout this report as it will form the bedrock of rebuilding our resilience including allowing realistic 
timeframes to deliver the transformation required for savings, cost reductions and financial sustainability.  
 
An application of £116m was submitted for support across the three years 2024-25 through to 2026-27 in 
declining amounts. Specifically, the EFS will support: 
 
• The rebuilding of General Balance reserves to the level recommended as prudent by the S151 
officer (estimated £40m) 
• Address historical issues in relation to bad debt provisions (estimated £16m) 
• Fund the current year underlying overspend and address one -off pressures ( £40m, of which 
£17.1m relates to the quarter 3 forecast, the balance in respect of a contingency relating to one-off 
pressures)  
• £20m to balance the 2026-27 budget.  
 
As is the case for the HRA, the aim is to fund the EFS from capital receipts generated from the disposal 
of assets in line with the capital strategy and strategic asset management framework as part of our wider 
work to deliver value for money for our residents and which are referenced later in this report. This reduces 
the burden on the revenue budget  for paying back and servicing increased debt.  Until such time as 
receipts are banked, we make provision for the revenue impact of borrowing to support the EFS in our 
Medium-Term Financial Strategy (MTFS). 
 
It is emphasised that the application for EFS is a one- off measure to restore our financial stability. This 
also assists us in responding directly to the concerns expressed by the External Auditor in their statutory 
recommendations of February 2025. We do not expect to apply for EFS in future years and will work hard

1. CONTEXT  
 
1.1 The budget and Council Tax are being set in the context of continuing to deal with well over a decade 
of structural underfunding and pressures on services , as well as sig nificant changes in the local 
government funding regime. While our previous plans had included more severe funding cuts than 
we now see, it  does point to a permanently reduced financial envelope. The Budget and MTFS 
ensure financial resources are align ed to the council’s priorities as set out in the Borough Plan – 
Lambeth 2030: Our Borough, Our Lambeth  
 
1.2 The January Finance Planning and Medium-Term Financial Strategy (2025-26 to 2029-30) Report 
to Cabinet gave an overview of the Government’s Autumn Statement as well as the Provisional Local 
Government Finance Settlement announced on 17 December 2025 . This report now updates that 
position for the Final Local Government Finance Settlement announced on 9th February 2026.  
 
Economic Outlook 
 
1.3 In its November 2025 economic and fiscal outlook , the Office of Budget Responsibility (OBR)  
projected that UK gross domestic product (GDP) will grow by 1.5% in 2025, then fall slightly to 1.4% 
in 2026, before settling at 1.5% to 2029-30. 
 
1.4 The latest reported consumer price inflation is 3.6% to December 2025. The OBR expect Consumer 
Price Index (CPI) inflation to average at 3.5% for 2025-26 before falling to 2.2% in 2026 -27 and 
return to the 2.0% target rate by 2027-28 partly due to the direct and indirect impact of the measures 
announced in the November Budget.  
 
1.5 The Bank of England’s ( BoE’s) base interest rate is 3.75% as at February 2026  after it voted to 
maintain interest rates in its February 2026 meeting. While inflation is above the 2% target, the Bank 
now expects it to fall t o target from April.  The Bank has indicated that the rate is likely to reduce 
further, and it is widely expected that the base rate will be reduced to 3%-3.25% by the end of 2026. 
Further detail is given in Appendix 1. 
 
London Context 
 
1.6 London Councils have reported that London boroughs are facing a £1bn budget shortfall compared 
to their original budget plans for this year (2025 -26). Homelessness represents the largest single 
driver of London boroughs’ current financial pressures, accounting  for £740m of their forecast 
overspend with Boroughs collectively spending around £5m each day on temporary accommodation. 
 
1.7 London is already the region most reliant on Exceptional Financial Support (EFS) to balance council 
budgets with six boroughs currently using EFS to balance core budgets. London Councils warned 
that this number could rise to 17, meaning up to half of London boroughs could require emergency 
support to avoid bankruptcy by 2028. 
 
Local Government Finance 
 
1.8 The Provisional Local Government Finance Settlement (PLGFS) was presented to Parliament on 17 
December 2025. This confirmed and provided details of many of the funding proposals from the 
Budget in November 2025 and the local government finance policy statement from 20 November 
2025. Final confirmation of 2026 -27 funding in the Local Government Finance Settlement was 
confirmed in a Written Statement in Parliament on 9 February 2026. The Final Settlement position 
was largely in line with the  provisional settlement with a small increase in transitional protection in

the form of the Recovery Grant uplift and more funding for homelessness. The final Council Tax and 
Business Rates baseline figures were agreed by Corporate Committee on 29 January 2026. 
 
1.9 While the final Local Government Finance settlement only provides details for a single financial year 
(2026-27), the provisional settlement also included 2027-28 and 2028-29, the remaining two years 
of the current Spending Review (SR25) period. This was our first clear view of the result of major 
reforms for local government funding, namely the Fair Funding Review 2.0  (FFR2.0) and the 
Business Rates Baseline Reset. FFR2.0 updated the share of national funding for each council and 
the Baseline Reset updated the amount of each council’s funding that is expected to come from 
locally retained business rates income.  
 
1.10 The provisional settlement showed that nationally Core Spending Power (CSP) will rise by £4.2bn 
in 2026-27, a further £2.3bn in 2027- 28 and then £3.6bn in 2028- 29, but this is heavily reliant on 
council tax income, through a combination of assumed tax base growth and maximum increases 
allowable under the referendum limit. Of the £11bn increase in CSP between 2025-26 and 2028-29, 
£8.3bn (75%) is expected to be funded through higher council tax income. 
 
1.11 Despite the average increases to CSP nationally, the impact of reforms on local authorities varies 
widely, with increases in CSP of nearly 30% for some councils compared to their 2025 -26 figure, 
and some councils with reductions of over 15%. This variation is also seen in the difference between 
outer and inner London boroughs, with outer London boroughs seeing average increases of 31.5% 
in Fair Funding Allocation but inner London boroughs like Lambeth with average reductions of 15.5% 
over the three years. 
 
1.12 Transitional Protection and Recovery is the mechanism the Government has used to smooth the 
transition from 2025 -26 funding levels to the new levels based on new shares. Most councils will 
have protection to 100% of their 2025-26 income made up of funding allocation, council tax (with 
growth assumptions), the grants that have been rolled into the funding allocation for 2026 -27 and 
the recovery grant. 
 
1.13 There is then further funding under the Recovery Grant Guarantee: a funding increase of 5% for 
CSP in 2026-27; another 1% in 2027-28 (i.e. 6% more than 2025-26); and a further 1% in 2028-29 
(i.e. 7% more than 2025-26), but with a cap of £35m in total allocated over the three years. Most 
councils are expected to reach this through their new funding allocations, but 22 councils will receive 
funding under the guarantee in at least one year of the settlement period, of those, four councils 
(Lambeth, Islington, Southwark and South Tyneside) will have their transitional funding limited by 
the £35m cap. 
 
1.14 Nationally, the final settlement for 2026-27 provided further transitional protection of £115m for 2026-
27, £150m in 2027- 28 and £175m in 2028 -29, totalling £450m over three years. There was also 
more funding under Homelessness, Rough Sleeping and Domestic Abuse grant.

Funding Allocation 
 
2.6 Lambeth’s funding allocation for 2026-27 is based on the new national shares as a result of FFR2.0 
which has seen a reduction of £17.5m compared to 2025- 26 after accounting for grants that were 
previously allocated under their own headings. This is mitigated in the short term by the continuation 
of the recovery grant, 100% income protection and the recovery grant guarantee. Together funding 
under this heading makes up 55.9% of total funding in 2026-27. 
 
Recovery Grant, 100% Income Protection and Recovery Grant Guarantee and Recovery 
Grant Uplift 
 
2.7 Funding under this heading helps to  smooth the impact of moving to new funding shares after the 
implementation of the new formula. In 2026- 27, this makes up 3.7% of total funding. While this is 
helpful for the 2026-27 budget position, it does not resolve long term budget pressures beyond the 
period of protection.  
 
Ringfenced Consolidated Grants 
 
2.8 The second strand of “funding simplification” brings together 16 previously separate funding streams 
into four ringfenced grants. As most of these were existing funding streams, Lambeth’s gains and 
losses compared to 2025- 26 will depend on the change between the old and new distribution 
method. 
 
2.9 Homelessness, Rough Sleeping and Domestic Abuse Grant is made up of the prevention and relief 
element (55%) of the Homelessness Prevention Grant, Rough Sleeping Accommodation 
Programme, Rough Sleeping Prevention and Recovery Grant, and Domestic Abuse Accommodation 
Support. Lambeth’s total allocation in 2025-26 for these funding streams was £9.085m and will fall 
to £8.889m in 2026-27. 
 
2.10 Children, Families and Youth Grant  is made up of the Children’s Social Care Prevention Grant, 
Supporting Families element of the Children and Families Grant, Holiday Activities and Food 
Programme, Post-16 Pupil Premium Plus Programme and new funding for Children’s Social Care 
Reform. Lambeth’s total allocation in 2025- 26 for these funding streams was £4.916m  and will 
increase to £6.191m in 2026-27. 
 
2.11 Crisis and Resilience Grant  brings together the successor to the Household Support Fund and 
Discretionary Housing Payments  (DHP). Lambeth’s total allocation in 2025- 26 for these funding 
streams was £5.747m, with £0.952m for Discretionary Housing Payments and £4.793m for the 
Household Support Fund. As the Household Support Fund was not expected to continue, it is not 
included in 2025-26 above for comparison purposes. The combined total in 2026-27 will be lower at 
£5.204m. The expectation is that the DHP element remains at the 2025-26 level. 
 
2.12 Public Health Grant adds the Drug and Alcohol Treatment and Recovery Improvement Grant, Local 
Stop Smoking Services and Support Grant and Swap to Stop programme to the Public Health Grant. 
This is a fully ringfenced specific grant so while included for completeness, it is ac counted for 
separately. Lambeth’s allocation for 2026-27 is £43.871m.

Council Tax Support Scheme 
 
2.16 For 2026-27, we are proposing a banded scheme where the level of support is based on: 
• income bands where people are grouped into a band according to their income; 
• household circumstance bands where households are grouped into a band according to 
the make-up of the household;

2.34 The borough’s housing stock is ageing and needs investment, with most homes being flats that are

over the next 30 years, including Lambeth's wider efforts to move towards its carbon net zero 
ambitions. Like many HRAs across London, Lambeth continues to face significant pressures

Housing General Fund 
 
3.6 Providing suitable temporary accommodation (TA) for households that are homeless and have a 
priority need is a statutory duty. The cost of TA has been highlighted in recent years as our main 
service pressure and has had a significant detrimental impact on our overall finances. It is noted 
though, that in recent months we have seen a downward trend in numbers and the introduction of 
initiatives that are having a positive impact. However, it is widely recognised that the current cost of 
temporary accommodation is unsustainable, both locally and nationally. Between 1 April 2023 and 
31 March 2024, Lambeth saw a 16% increase in households needing temporary accommodation. In 
addition, the rates paid for these placements increased by 14%, resulting in a 30% increase in 
expenditure for Temporary Accommodation. 
 
3.7 This demand and increase in rates continued into 2024-25 before policies were implemented to limit 
the rates of new Temporary Accommodation and discharge homeless families into Private Rented 
properties. This stabilised the expenditure growth but resulted in the 2024- 25 outturn exceeding 
£100m of expenditure on Temporary Accommodation. 
  
3.8 Temporary accommodation income from Housing Benefit and rents is based on 2011 Local Housing 
Allowance and so has not kept pace with the growth in expenditure over the past 13 years. Income 
from TA rents funds less than 40% of the cost of the accommodation. In 2024- 25 the £100m of 
expenditure was only matched with £40m of rental income resulting in a shortfall for the Council. 
Initiatives on prevention and move-on are aiming to reduce the numbers of families coming into TA 
and enabling them to move into sec ure housing more quickly, but there are  limited affordable 
alternatives within London.

forecast cost of TA in April 2025 was higher, at £115m. However, with continued initiatives in 
prevention and move on, and reducing the rates paid for TA, the 2025-26 expenditure has been held 
at £105m. The reductions in spend in this year have a positive impact on 2026-27 where for the start 
of 2026-27, it is forecast the full year cost in April will have reduced to £90m, with further reductions 
planned to be achieved to come in on budget by the end of 2026-27.

10. EQUALITIES IMPACT ASSESSMENT  
 
Our legal duties 
 
10.1 Under the Public Sector Equality Duty of Equality Act 2010, decision makers must evidence 
consideration of any potential impacts of their proposals on people who share the protected 
characteristics, before decisions are taken. This includes any decisions r elating to how authorities 
act as employers; how they develop, evaluate and review policies; how they design, deliver, and 
evaluate services, and also how they commission and procure services from others.  
 
10.2 Section 149 of the Act requires public bodies to have due regard to the need to: 
• eliminate unlawful discrimination, harassment, victimisation and any other conduct 
prohibited by the Act. 
• advance equality of opportunity between people who share a protected characteristic 
and people who do not share it; and 
• foster good relations between people who share a protected characteristic and people 
who do not share it. 
 
Protected characteristics defined by law include race and ethnicity, disability, sex, gender 
reassignment, age, sexual orientation, pregnancy and maternity, and religion or belief. Having due 
regard means that we are required to consciously think about the three tenets of the Equality Duty 
within our decision-making processes. By law, our assessments must contain sufficient information 
to enable a public authority to show it has paid ‘due regard’ to the equalities duties and identified 
methods for mitigating or avoiding any adverse impact on people sharing protected characteristics.
