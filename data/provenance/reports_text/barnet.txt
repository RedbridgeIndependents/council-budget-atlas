Summary 
This report sets out the proposed budget for 2026/27 and the council’s Medium Term Financial Strategy (MTFS) 
which include the assumptions for 2026-31 with regard to council tax, other funding, expenditure forecasts, and 
the savings and income generation plans necessary to set a balanced budget. This helps to ensure resources are 
aligned to the strategic outcomes set out in Our Plan for Barnet.  
 
The council continues to face increasing demand for services such as social care and temporary accommodation. 
To tackle the impact of these challenges in 2025/26 and beyond, we engaged an Improvement Partner, Peopletoo, 
to work with the council to identify further opportunities for reducing service demand, make savings, improve 
efficiency and increase income generation. This work took place alongside our ongoing review of in-year and 
future service pressures, as well as the council’s continuing programme of transformation activity, including our 
work on digital transformation.  
 
The Provisional Local Government Finance Settlement (PLGFS), which included the outcome of the Government’s 
Fair Funding Reforms, was received on 17th December 2025.  The MTFS presented to Council includes the outcome 
of the PLGFS, the Final Local Government Finance Settlement (FLGFS), and best estimates of other income and 
expenditure. 
 
The revised MTFS shows a budget gap of £79.3m for 2026/27, including identified savings. Services, working 
alongside the Improvement Partner, have identified savings and income generation proposals of £9.4m leaving 
the unfunded gap of £79.3m.  For 2026-2031 the MTFS shows the budget gap increasing across the MTFS period 
due to cost pressures with a forecast budget gap of £244m in 2031. This is after total savings identified of £19.4m 
and includes total Exceptional Financial Support (EFS) Financing costs of £69.5m (assuming EFS is approved to 
fund the budget gap in each year). 
 
Despite continued efforts to reduce pressures and identify savings and income generating opportunities, there 
remains a 15% budget gap for 2026/27.  As previously reported to Cabinet, we have sought assistance from 
Government in the form of an application for EFS to help achieve a balanced budget position for 2026/27. 
Following a further improvement to our funding in the Final Local Government Finance Settlement (FLGFS) the 
amount of EFS applied for has been adjusted from £88.4m to £79.6m. 
 
The council received confirmation on 22 nd February 2026 that the Minster of State for Local Government and 
Homelessness is minded to approve a capitalisation direction of a total not exceeding £79.6 million for 2026/27. 
 
This Exceptional Financial Support will take the form of approval from the Government that the council is able to, 
in the short term, borrow funds and use capital receipts to support revenue expenditure.  Any borrowing of funds 
has, in itself, revenue implications in the form of interest payments and Minimum Revenue Provision which must 
be put aside for the future repayment of any loan undertaken. These revenue impacts have been built into the 
MTFS. 
 
The approval of Exceptional Financial Support gives the council time to develop and implement proposals to 
significantly reduce spend and increase income. EFS is not, and is not intended to be, a long-term solution to the 
council’s financial position.

Council Tax – impact of FFR 
2.14 Under the FFR, the government have used a “notional” council tax, which is effectively an assumed (or 
notional) level of council tax. It has been used to assess each local authority’s relative ability to raise income 
locally and is a standardised national figure, usually set at the average Band D council tax in England. 
 
2.15 The notional council tax has been used in the resource allocation workings of the FFR and funding has been 
based on: 
• A council’s relative need, i.e. what it needs to deliver services; and 
• Relative resources, which includes how much a council could raise through council tax if everyone 
charged the same “notional” council tax amount. 
 
2.16 This means that the FFR assumes that: 
• Councils with stronger tax bases – i.e. with more homes in Bands E upwards - should be able to 
raise more income locally and therefore receive less grant; and  
• Those with lower than the “notional” council tax rate should also be able to raise more income 
locally and receive less grant.  
 
2.17 As the council has a large tax base and has historically maintained one of the lowest council tax rates in 
London, we have a lower council tax yield per household than many authorities of a similar size.  
 
2.18 As a result, the use of the notional council tax in the resource allocation of the FFR has had a detrimental 
impact on our funding which gives rise to two compounding risks: 
• Lower Central Government Support - The FFR has deemed that Barnet should be able to raise 
more income locally due to its tax base size and low council tax rate compared to the “notional” 
level. 
• Higher Local Tax Pressure Going Forward - future council tax rises must be larger and more 
frequent to maintain financial stability and keep pace with current and future demand pressures.

2.33 A summary of the changes between the provisional and final settlement is given below:  
➢ Business rates pools – the treatment of pooling gains has changed in the final settlement. As a 
result, the RSG, Recovery Guarantee and funding floor allocations have changed for authorities in 
business rates pools.   
• The original approach for assigning extra business rates achieved through pooling was based 
on an assessment of levy liability for relevant authorities in the absence of a pool.  This 
meant that the entire pooling gain was assigned to tariff authority members. 
• The revised approach allocates 50% of the pooling gain to top-up authorities within each 
pool, based on their relative size of the baseline funding level, while the remaining 50% is 
shared between tariff authorities in pools using the previous approach.  
• As a results, tariff authorities in pools see a reduction to their Fair Funding Allocations and 
top-up authorities in pools will receive the positive effect of this in their 2026/27 without any 
mitigation.  
• These changes have been made within Revenue Support Grant and, as Barnet is a top-up 
authority, this has resulted in a £1.480m increase in RSG compared to Provisional Settlement 
figures.  
➢ The consolidated Homelessness, Rough Sleeping and Domestic Abuse Grant has been increased by 
£92 million by 2028/29 compared to 2025/26. Approximately 40% of the uplifted is aimed at 
Renters’ Rights new burdens and the rest should be targeted at supported housing. For Barnet, this 
results in an additional £0.58m compared to the Provisional Settlement. 
 
2.34 Other changes which did not impact Barnet are:  
➢ Funding floor – changes to pooling arrangements and the treatment of the City of London have 
reduced the cost of the funding floor in all 3 years of the settlement. This reduction in funding floor 
cost has freed up funding which has been used to uplift the Recovery Grant.  
➢ Recovery Grant uplift - over three years, a further £440 million of Recovery Grant has been 
allocated to those councils in receipt of it. The increase is £115m in 2026/27, £150m in 2027/28 
and £175m in 2028/29.  
➢ SEND deficits – the Government has committed to resolving 90% of local authorities’ DSG High 
Needs deficits accrued to the end of 2025-26. All local authorities with a SEND deficit will be 
eligible to receive grant funding subject to submitting and securing the Department for Education’s 
approval of a local SEND reform plan. From 2028/29, SEND spending will be covered by the overall 
government DEL budget, meaning local authorities are not expected to fund future SEND costs 
from general funds, once the Statutory Override ends at the end of 2027/28.  
➢ Council tax – referendum limits have not been set for six authorities with the lowest council tax 
levels.  In addition, MHCLG have agreed small additional flexibilities for seven councils in 2026/27.  
➢ No changes to funding allocations for consolidated grants were announced but MHCLG have 
committed to publishing final allocations for consolidated grants by Friday 13 February.

Pressures 
3.5 The council has, over many years and alongside the whole local government sector, faced growing financial 
pressures. These have arisen from several key trends including: 
➢ Rising quantity of demand for some statutory services, notably for older people and for adults and 
children with disabilities. 
➢ Rising complexity of demand in some statutory services, notably for adults and children with 
disabilities. 
➢ Rapidly rising external unit costs, especially in children’s social care placements. 
➢ Rising Temporary Accommodation (TA) pressures, driven by rising homelessness and escalating 
costs. 
 
3.6 Officers have undertaken a thorough review of in-year 2025/26 pressures to identify which are expected to 
continue into future years as well as quantified new expected pressures that may materialise in 2026/27 
and future years. 
 
3.7 The most significant movement has been the increase in both demand and costs of providing social care 
services, addressing homelessness, and meeting the shortfall caused by housing benefit rates that have not

been updated since 2011, as well as the financing costs of EFS.  Acknowledging and providing for this is vital 
to enable the Council to continue to provide services to the most vulnerable requiring extra support and to 
help both manage risk and prevent escalation of need. 
 
➢ Adult Social Care (ASC) – ASC demand modelling follows an evidence-based approach which 
combines Office for National Statistics (ONS) population projections as well as Projecting Older 
People Population Information (POPPI) and Projecting Adult Needs and Service Information (PANSI) 
data, across all age groups to derive future demand and costs for the service up to 2030.  The big 
pressure drivers for Communities, Adults & Health (CAH) are: 
o Demographic growth and increase in complexity,  
o Increased demand within Transitions (18-25) service,  
o Provider inflation (additional amount required over and above modelled in MTFS), and  
o Increased cost of providing community equipment following the collapse of the previous 
provider.  
 
➢ Children’s Social Care - Demand in Children’s and Family Services (CFS) is forecast using a 
combination of: 
o Existing demand in the system, adjusted for prudent expectations of managing this down 
through strategies such as regular placement review panels 
o Expectations of new demand based on historical trends, adjusted for new drivers such as 
changes to Government welfare support and indices of deprivation 
 
➢ Temporary Accommodation & Housing Benefit Subsidy Loss - The Barnet Group (TBG) have 
modelled future demand based upon a supply and demand model, with known and projected supply 
combined with known demand (e.g. re-generation decants) and expected future demand.  This has 
been fed into the TBG TA costs model that translates that supply and demand into costs based upon 
current TA costs as well as factoring in expected inflation based upon recent and long-term trends. 
o Temporary Accommodation costs have seen an unprecedented increase over the last two 
years. The net cost of TA (i.e. TA costs less rental income) has increased due to demand and

Reserves 
3.59 The council holds reserves to deal with future pressures where the value or the timing of the pressure is 
uncertain, or where the funding can only be spent on specific objectives (e.g., grant funding). Reserves are 
divided into ‘ringfenced’ reserves, where the spending objective is known with some clarity, and 
’committed’ reserves, intended to mitigate the impact of wholly unforeseeable costs. The levels of reserves 
are set out under Section 25 of the Local Government Act and prudent levels are determined by the Chief 
Finance Officer (CFO). Ringfenced reserves are usually held by specific services, while committed reserves

4 S25 Statement: View of Section 151 Officer 
Introduction 
4.1 In order to comply with Section 25(1) of the Local Government Act 2003, the Section 151 Officer (the 
Executive Director of Finance) is required to provide their professional opinion on: 
1. the robustness of the estimates made for the purposes of the budget calculations; and 
2. the adequacy of the Council’s proposed reserves. 
 
4.2 Section 25(2) of the same Act requires the authority to have regard to this report of the Section 151 officer 
when making decisions about the budget and the level of council tax.  
 
4.3 This statement is provided to support Members in their consideration and approval of the 2026/27 Budget 
and Medium Term Financial Strategy (MTFS). 
 
2025/26 Financial Position and Effectiveness of Budget Management 
4.4 The council has robust arrangements in place for managing budgets and performance. Close attention is 
paid to increasing service and demand pressures, implementation of agreed savings and monitoring of the 
council’s in-year financial position, with regular reporting to Cabinet and Overview & Scrutiny Committee. 
 
4.5 The Q3 forecast for 2025/26 is a balanced position after the use of contingency. However, the council’s 
2025/26 budget was balanced on the basis of using £55.7m of Exceptional Financial Support. This means 
that while the council has been able to keep expenditure within approved budgets, there is a built-in deficit 
which needs to be  addressed over the medium term.  
 
4.6 Reserves balances were low going into 2025/26 and the total reserves balance, as at the end of 2025/26 is 
estimated to be £50.4m, with General Fund balances of £15m.  
 
Robustness of estimates made for the purpose of the budget preparation and assurance from the Chief 
Finance Officer 
4.7 Developing the budget estimates for a given financial year is an ongoing, iterative process within the 
medium-term financial planning cycle. This is a council-wide process involving all spending departments 
whereby estimates are worked up, challenged, and refined as further information becomes available. It 
considers the most recently available budget monitoring information and the latest assumptions for the 
forthcoming financial year. In particular, the proposed savings have been reviewed, challenged and signed 
off as deliverable by key stakeholders across the organisation. The thoroughness of this process is a source 
of assurance in determining that overall estimates in the budget are robust. 
 
4.8 Budget estimates are exactly that – estimates of spending and income made at a point in time. This 
statement about the robustness of estimates cannot give a guaranteed assurance about the budget but 
instead gives Members reasonable assurances that the budget has been based on the best available 
information and assumptions.  
 
4.9 The council has undertaken a robust process to produce its MTFS to address the changing financial 
landscape and on-going budget pressures. These processes are necessary to ensure all budget proposals 
are: 
• aligned to Our Plan for Barnet; 
• fully evaluated for any legal, HR equality and procurement issues; 
• assessed thoroughly to ensure if stakeholder consultations are needed and if so to ensure these 
are completed in time; and 
• appropriately challenged to ensure they are feasible.

2026/27 Budget 
4.16 Barnet’s financial position entering 2026/27 remains extremely challenging. The Council continues to face: 
• Escalating demand pressures, particularly within Children’s Social Care, Adults’ Social Care, SEND, 
and homelessness/temporary accommodation; 
• Persistent inflation, particularly in care markets, utilities, and contractual services; 
• Significant workforce pressures, including recruitment and retention challenges in statutory 
services; 
• Structural budget gaps emerging from demand outstripping available resources;

• Limited opportunities for further efficiencies without material service impacts; and 
• While Fair Funding 2.0 delivered small increases in the council’s grant funding, these increases  do 
not cover the projected cost of providing services. 
 
4.17 Despite actions taken in previous years, the intensity and scale of these pressures have continued, and in 
some areas increased, into 2026/27. 
 
4.18 The proposed budget reflects the outcome of the Government’s Fair Funding Reforms and latest 
Government grants for 2026/27 as published in the Final Local Government Finance Settlement on 9th  
February 2026.  It also allows for pay and non-pay inflation and best estimates of on-going service pressures.  
 
4.19 Allowance for these pressures has resulted in a significant budget gap of £79.3m for 2026/27. Savings and 
Income generation proposals of £9.4m have been identified to help plug this gap but nevertheless, there 
remains a gap. 
 
4.20 As a result, the council has applied for EFS and has received confirmation from MHCLG that they are minded 
to approve our application for Exceptional Financial Support (EFS) for 2026/27. This Exceptional Financial 
Support takes the form of approval from the government that the council is able to, in the short term, either 
identify assets for disposal, use capital receipts or, as a last resort, borrow to support revenue expenditure.   
 
4.21 The Executive Director of Growth, in discussion with CMT and members, has kicked off a Strategic Asset 
Management Plan in order to review the council’s asset base to generate capital receipts from the sale of 
assets. These capital receipts could then be used to fund revenue spend in 2026/27 thereby reducing the 
amount of borrowing required for EFS. 
 
4.22 Exceptional Financial Support is not a long-term solution and therefore the Section 151 Officer recommends 
that this step is taken only as a last resort and all actions should be taken to reduce the need for EFS both 
now and in the future as it does not address structural funding issues. 
 
Future Years 
4.23 Barnet’s financial position is challenging as set out in this report. Although the council legally only has to set 
a one-year budget for the year ahead, it is important that we continue to have a strong focus on medium 
term planning and setting multi-year budgets. The PLGFS gave details of 3-year local government funding 
settlements from 2026/27, it is possible additional grants are available in subsequent years but for now, it 
shows that the council has a significant hill to climb in order to get back to financial sustainability.   
 
4.24 The S151 Officer can confirm that the council is taking measures to ensure that its future financial 
sustainability is viable by investing in prevention, housing and digital and data transformation, reducing 
costs in demand led services like social care and homelessness and working alongside neighbouring 
boroughs and London Councils to ensure best practice is being adopted. 
 
Exceptional Financial Support (EFS) 
4.25 The council is able to set a balanced budget for 2026/27, but only with additional EFS from MHCLG. This 
reflects the ongoing fragility of the Council’s financial position. 
 
4.26 An application was submitted to MHCLG for EFS and confirmation that MHCLG are minded to approve our 
application has been received. This means that the council has a Capitalisation Direction which gives 
permission to fund day to day running costs of up to £79.6m through the capital programme.

4.27 If the Council has to borrow, this has revenue implications in the form of interest payments and Minimum 
Revenue Provision which must be put aside for the future repayment of any loan undertaken. These 
revenue impacts have been built into the MTFS. 
 
4.28 Receipt of EFS is a clear indication that the Council’s underlying financial position is not sustainable without 
Government intervention. While EFS provides temporary flexibility—primarily through capitalisation, it 
does not address the fundamental drivers of the budget gap.  
 
4.29 EFS should be regarded as intended to provide an “interim measure” whilst the Council develops its longer-
term financial strategy and wider national and regional policy issues are addressed. Reliance on EFS is not 
a financially sustainable strategy.  
 
Adequacy of the Council’s proposed reserves 
Robustness of Balances and Reserves 
4.30 The council is required to maintain an adequate level of reserves to deal with unexpected events and 
pressures. Sections 31 (A) and 42 (A) of the Local Government Finance Act 1992 requires the council to have 
regard to the level of reserves when calculating the budget requirement. 
 
4.31 The appropriate level of reserves must be considered alongside an assessment of risk, taking into account 
the robustness of savings plans, levels of risk in estimates in demand and price and wider economic factors. 
The adequacy of reserves is assessed as part of the budget process and monitored regularly through the 
year to the closure of accounts at the end of the year. 
 
4.32 The longer-term sustainability of the council relies on the need to avoid reliance on reserves in the future 
and to have a longer-term plan to replenish reserves available for managing risks and uncertainties. Across 
the medium term there will need to be a planned replenishment of reserves to get reserves to a more 
sustainable financial position but this will have to happen after the council is no longer in EFS.   
 
4.33 It is the recommendation of the Section 151 Officer that the General Fund reserve should not be reduced 
below the current targeted level of £15m and that as soon as practically possible (i.e. when the council is 
financially sustainable) that there is a planned reserves contribution to increase them to a minimum of 
£40m.

11 Consulting and Engagement 
11.1 As a matter of public law, the duty to consult on proposals to vary, reduce or withdraw services will arise 
in four circumstances: 
➢ where there is a statutory requirement in the relevant legislative framework; 
➢ where the practice has been to consult, or, where a policy document states the council will 
consult, then the council must comply with its own practice or policy; 
➢ exceptionally, where the matter is so important that there is a legitimate expectation of 
consultation; 
➢ where consultation is required to complete an equalities impact assessment. 
 
11.2 Consultation is also recommended in other circumstances, for example to identify the impact of proposals 
or to assist with complying with the council’s equalities duties. 
 
11.3 Regardless of whether the council has a duty to consult, if it chooses to consult, such consultation must 
be carried out fairly. In general, a consultation can only be considered as proper consultation if: 
➢ Comments are genuinely invited at the formative stage; 
➢ The consultation documents include sufficient reasons for the proposal to allow those being 
consulted to be properly informed and to give an informed response; 
➢ There is adequate time given to the consultees to consider the proposals; 
➢ There is a mechanism for feeding back the comments and those comments are considered by 
the decision-maker / decision-making body when making a final decision; 
➢ The degree of specificity with which, in fairness, the public authority should conduct its 
consultation exercise may be influenced by the identity of those whom it is consulting;  
➢ Where relevant and appropriate, the consultation is clear on the reasons why and extent to 
which alternatives and discarded options have been discarded. The more intrusive the decision, 
the more likely it is to attract a higher level of procedural fairness.

12 Equalities and Diversity 
 
12.1 Equality and diversity issues are a mandatory consideration in the decision-making of the council. 
 
12.2 Decision makers should have due regard to the public-sector equality duty in making their decisions. The 
equalities duties are continuing duties they are not duties to secure a particular outcome. The equalities 
impact will be revisited on each of the proposals as they are developed. Consideration of the duties should 
precede the decision. It is important that Council has regard to the statutory grounds in the light of all 
available material such as consultation responses. The statutory grounds of the public-sector equality 
duty are found at section 149 of the Equality Act 2010 and are as follows below. 
 
12.3 A public authority must, in the exercise of its functions, have due regard to the need to: 
a. eliminate discrimination, harassment, victimisation and any other conduct that is prohibited by 
or under this Act; 
b. advance equality of opportunity between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic 
and persons who do not share it; 
c. Foster good relations between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic and 
persons who do not share it. 
 
12.4 Having due regard to the need to advance equality of opportunity between persons who share a relevant 
protected characteristic and persons who do not share it involves having due regard, in particular, to the 
need to: 
d. remove or minimise disadvantages suffered by persons who share a relevant protected 
characteristic that are connected to that characteristic; 
e. take steps to meet the needs of persons who share a relevant protected characteristic that are 
different from the needs of persons who do not share it; 
f. Encourage persons who share a relevant protected characteristic to participate in public life or 
in any other activity in which participation by such persons is disproportionately low. 
 
12.5 The steps involved in meeting the needs of disabled persons that are different from the needs of persons 
who are not disabled include, in particular, steps to take account of disabled persons’ disabilities. 
 
12.6 Having due regard to the need to foster good relations between persons who share a relevant protected 
characteristic and persons who do not share it involves having due regard, in particular, to the need to: 
g. Tackle prejudice, and 
h. Promote understanding.
