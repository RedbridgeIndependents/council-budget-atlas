SUMMARY 
This report presents the overall financial position of the City Fund (i.e. the City 
Corporation’s finances relating to Local Government, Police and Port Health services). 
 
Following the Local Government Settlement on the 9 February 2026, whilst the 
medium-term outlook is balanced, there is fundamental uncertainty in core 
government funding from 2029/30 onwards which the City Corporation needs to 
address. Following consi derable discussion with  His Majesty’s  Government, a 
bespoke arrangement has been reached, recognising the unique circumstances of the 
City Corporation. This provides confirmed transitional funding for 2026/27, with 
indicative transitional allocations for 2 027/28 and 2028/29, enabling a balanced 
position across the medium term. However, the relief provided, whilst addressing 
funding needs across the medium term, is purely transitional and the core government 
funding uncertainty remains. Addressing the longer -term arrangements will require 
both political advocacy for appropriate funding levels for the statutory services that are 
provided for London and nation and a radical approach to the services that the City 
Corporation provides from its local government fund.  
 
To date, discussions with government have focused, and will continue to focus, on 
how the services that the City Corporation provides pan -London and Nation -wide, 
many under Statue, are to be financed – e.g. Port Authority, Animal Health & Welfare 
and grants to the London Museum, costs which would otherwise fall on other public 
bodies. Modelling indicates an additional c£39m p.a . — plus c£500m for forthcoming 
capital expenditure — is required for the City Fund to deliver these services at break 
even.  The current funding formula does not capture the cost of delivering these 
services due to the unique nature of them to the City Corporation. 
 
Government has also been clear that the City Corporation is expected to use its tax -
setting flexibilities to meet the cost of services driven by substantial daily population 
and this report recommends both increases in council tax and the business rates 
premium accordingly. At the recent ratepayers’ and residents meeting, proposals to 
increase the Business Rate Premium and Council Tax were presented, and no 
opposition was raised. While this will provide additional support for daytime population 
driven pressures, it will not close the funding gap in full. 
 
The key immediate risk remains the fragility of the Housing Revenue Account and the 
Chamberlain’s s25 report highlights that reserves are extremely low and has a key 
dependency on both the Housing Revenue Account (HRA) recovery plan and approval 
of the exceptional financial support requested from government to ensure compliance 
with regulations and statutory fiscal duties.

While the reset of the business rates system in 2026/27 may create opportunities for 
growth from 2027/28, it cannot be depended upon to fund ongoing business as usual 
service pressures, reinforcing the need to address the structural funding gap through 
radical decisions. 
 
Priority decisions will be required by the City Corporation, including difficult choices 
about ceasing or scaling  back certain activities, alongside a review of discretionary 
spending and service delivery models. In parallel, opportunities for efficiency gains, 
shared services, and digital transformation should be accelerated to reduce cost 
pressures and improve resilience.  
 
On policing, the final settlement for 2026/27 was confirmed on 28 January 2026, 
following publication of the Government ’s White Paper on Police Reform, which sets 
out an ambitious reform programme for policing. The 2026/27 settlement provides only 
one-year funding, including funding for 13 additional Neighbourhood Police Officers 
under the Neighbourhood Policing Guarantee (NPG). Although the extra funding 
allocated to the NPG does not fully meet the associated cost increases, the 
frontloading of the three-year Spending Review settlement makes this this affordable 
in 2026/27. However, this approach increases financial risk in later years. The Force 
is also awaiting confirmation of grant funding for its national responsibilities in fraud, 
economic crime and cyber -crime, and this uncertainty continues to pose challenges 
for forward planning.

MAIN REPORT 
 
Background 
1.0 This report sets out the revenue and capital budgets for City Fund for the Court 
of Common Council to approve.  
 
2.0 The budget setting process for 2026/27 and beyond began back in May 2025 
with a series of officer-led star chamber meetings focusing on key pressure and 
opportunities across departments including the Barbican Centre , Environment, 
Community and Children’s Services, and the Housing Revenue Account. These 
meetings reviewed pressures and potential savings within each service area.  
Several common pressure areas were identified, including London Living Wage 
increases, ongoing pressures from the 2025/26 period that may contribute, and 
impact on enabling services due to the scale of demand.  
 
Economic Stability and Government Actions: 
 
3.0 After a prolonged period of  high inflation  and economic volatility , national 
conditions are beginning to  stabilise. The Consumer Price Index ( CPI) is 
forecast to fall around 2.2% in 2026, providing some relief for households and 
public services. However, the impact of the recent inflationary spike  remains 
embedded across wages, service contracts, and supply chains  continuing to 
drive up public sector costs. Economic growth is projected to remain weak, with 
UK GDP expected to increase by only around 1.0% in 2026. This subdued 
environment, combined with rising demand for services such as housing, social 
care, and welfare support will continue to place pressure on loc al government 
finances.  

=== PDF PAGE 9 ===
 
4.0 There remains a n otable degree of uncertainty and risk surrounding the 
economic forecast for 2026 , influenced by a range of external and domestic 
factors. While the labour market has shown signs of softening, significant global 
events such as geopolitical tensions and economic policies in other countries 
contribute to pose potential economic instability. The impact could have a 
significant consequence  on Ci ty Fund  – increasing service pressures and 
demand; reduction in business rates; pressures on income streams; capital 
programme affordability.  
 
5.0 Against this backdrop the planned changes to Local Government funding, the 
Final Local Government Funding settle ment, released on 9 th February 2026, 
confirms that the City Fund will receive three years of transitional support at the 
level sought by the City Corporation, though this remains subject to annual 
review and does not reflect assessment of core need. While this provides short-
term stability, it d efers significant underlying financial pressures, particularly 
given that business rates growth cannot be used to support day to day services 
and must instead be prioritised for addressing historic underinvestment in the 
operational estate. The City Corporation will, therefore, need to continue closing 
the structural financial gap to ensure long-term sustainability.  
 
6.0 The Police Funding Settlement for 2026/27 was confirmed on 29 January 2026 
and resulted in a one -year settlement including an increase of 13 
Neighbourhood Police Officers as part of the Government’s Neighbourhood 
Policing Guarantee (NPG). While the additional funding made available for the 
NPG does not cover the increase in cost, the frontloading of the 3 -year 
Spending Review settlement enables it to be affordable in 2026/27 but will add 
risk in subsequent years. The City of London Police (CoLP) is still awaiting 
confirmation of grant funding for 2026/27 and beyond for its national work on 
fraud, economic and cyber-crime. This delay is creating significant challenges 
for planning in the context of the uncertainty and risk arising from the White 
Paper on Police Reform publish ed on 26 January  2026, with which the CoLP 
and Police Authority Board are engaged jointly. 
 
7.0 While individual budgets have undergone changes, some overarching 
messages from the 2025/26 MTFP remain consistent.  Those being: 
• City Fund is expected to fall into deficit within the MTFP period. 
• The scale of the capital programme and major projects is placing significant 
pressure on the resources available.   
 
Current Position 
 
8.0 The Provisional Local Government Settlement announced on 17 December 
2025 set out both the revised assessment of needs and indicated the City 
Corporation would receive a one -year funding settlement for 2026/27. Most 
significantly, the Government confirmed the reset of the funding system, 
including changes to central grant allocations and business rates redistribution. 
Under the revised needs assessment, the City Corporation’s central 
government funding falls sharply from £77m to £18m , driven largely by 
deprivation-based metrics that do not reflect the significant impact of c680,000 

=== PDF PAGE 10 ===
daily commuters. During the consultation period,  the Corporation was formally 
recognised as requiring a bespoke funding arrangement due to its unique 
operating context. As a result, the Final Local Government Settlement released 
on 9th February introduced a shift in funding certainty. The Corporation has been 
awarded a three-year transitional settlement in line with other Local Authorities. 
This provides confirmed  transitional funding for 2026/27, with indicative 
transitional allocations for 2027/28 and 2028/29.  
 
9.0 While this extended transitional relief is good news, it only offers short -term 
stability it does not resolve the longer-term challenge. A substantial funding cliff-
edge remains from 2029/30 onwards , when transitional support end s, leaving 
the financial outlook uncertain if the current funding formula continues to 
underpin allocations beyond this point. Continued lobbying with the Ministry of 
Housing, Communities and Local Government (MHCLG) will therefore be 
essential to confirm the indica tive allocations and establish a sustainable long-
term funding position beyond the current spending period.

focus on, how the services that the City Corporation provides for pan-London 
and Nation-wide, many under Statue, are to be financed – e.g. Port Authority, 
Animal Health & Welfare and grants to the London Museum, costs which would 
otherwise fall on other public bodies. Modelling indicates an additional c£39m 
p.a. — plus c£500m for forthcoming capital expenditure — is required for the 
City Fund to deliver these services at break even.  The current funding formula 
does not capture the cost of delivering these services due to the unique nature 
of them to the City Corporation.  
 
11.0 Government has also been clear that the City Corporation is expected to use its 
tax-setting flexibilities to meet the cost of services driven by substantial daily 
population. At the recent ratepayers’ meeting, proposals to increase the 
Business Rate Premium were presented, and no opposition was raised. While 
this will provide additional support for daytime population driven pressures, it will 
not close the funding gap in full. 
 
Financial Challenges: 
 
12.0 Whilst this report focuses on budget setting for 2026/27, substantive financial 
challenges lay in using the transitional funding period to 2028/29 to establish a 
sustainable ongoing budget against a backdrop of reduced funding. Transitional 
relief provided during 2026/27 to 2028/29 totals c£150m but needs to be held to 
smooth projected deficits and capital pressures rather than committed for 
individual projects.  Despite this transitional funding, the underlying principle 
remains that governments assessment  of needs for the  City Corporation is far 
lower than current funding required for the services it delivers and generates 
significant uncertainty for our local authority services and those provided for pan-
London and Nation-wide within City Fund.  
 
13.0 Significant work and resources have been dedicated to the Housing Revenue 
Account (HRA) as part of 2026/27 budget setting.  This includes £152m of 
Capital Funding resources from City Fund, as well as a request for Exceptional

Financial Support (EFS) for a £2.6m ca pitalisation direction to strengthen the 
HRA’s financial position in 2026/27 , which is yet to be confirmed . These

remains under significant pressure and fiscal sustainability is extremely fragile. 
The Chamberlain’s s25 report highlights that the HRA Reserves are extremely 
low and a key dependency on the success of the HRA recovery plan to avoid 
breaching statutory fiscal duties.

within the Final Local Government Settlement for 2027/28 and 2028/29 in core

separate consultation process, there is a chance these figures will change in

services relating to daytime population for the next four years. This deficit 
therefore represents the level of savings required across all other services 
provided under City Fund. However, this report recommends utilising transitional 
relief alongside I&E surplus reserves to address these deficits over the medium-
term. It further proposes a specific allocation of transitional relief to be reserved 
for operational property renovations . The surplus arising in 2025/26 provides 
scope to balance the position in both 2027/28 and 2028/29, mitigating the short-
term pressure. While reserves offer short -term flexibility, usable reserves are 
forecast to reduce sharply over the MTFP period and cannot provide a 
sustainable solution to addressing ongoing structural revenue pressures.  
 
15.0 While the reset of the business rates system in 2026/27 may create opportunities 
for growth from 2027/28, it is anticipated that the Greater London Authority will 
retain approximately one -third of any additional income generated under its 
current agreement. In addition, Government has indicat ed an intention to 
undertake further resets of the system, which is likely to happen between three 
to six years, limiting the period over which the City can retain any gains. When 
combined with the inherent constraints on growing business rates and propert y 
values within a highly developed and geographically fixed area, this significantly 
restricts the City’s ability to rely on business rates growth as a sustainable 
funding source. As a result, business rates growth cannot be depended upon to 
fund ongoing b usiness as usual service pressures, reinforcing the need to 
address the structural funding gap through radical decisions.  
 
16.0 The changes to local government funding and business rate retention mean that 
the previously established ‘8 Authority Pool’ will cease to operate after 2025/26.  
This arrangement is forecast to generate £17.5m for City Fund in 2025/26, and 
as it has always been treated as one -off funding and not used to support 
business as usual, there is no impact on future year forecasts.

31.2 Martyn’s Law, formally the Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, 
mandates stricter security requirements for public venues, including courts. 
The legislation aims to improve preparedness and prevention of terrorist 
attacks, ensure public safety, mandating enhanced security measures and 
technologies. While primarily targeting arenas and shopping centres, it also 
affects justice sector. These measures are essential not only for the 
protection of individuals, i.e. judges, staff, litigants and the public, but also 
for the integrity and efficiency of the legal proceedings.  Compliance 
necessitates strengthened access controls, enhanced physical and 
technology protections aligned to prevailing threat assessments. 
31.3 The introduction of additional income from the Business Rates multiplier, 
resulting in an increase in Business Rates Premium, along with Martyn’s 
Law taking effect on 3rd April 2025, presents an opportunity to reprioritise 
funding for security -related expenses at the Courts within the Salisbury 
Square Development (SSD) major projects. This could ease the financial 
burden on City’s Estate, which is currently providing a £210m grant to the 
City Fund for building the Courts at SSD. At present, City’s Estate must sell 
investment assets, leading to an estimated annual reduction in rental 
income of £8.4m p.a. The total security costs fo r the Courts are estimated 
to be £41m. The change in funding arrangements enables the City’s Estate 
to retain c£1.6m in annual rental income  or redirect £41m to other 
forthcoming major programmes, as highlighted in the City’s Estate 2026/27 
budget and MTFP paper. 
31.4 For 2026/27 this will result in a reduction of c£7m grant from City’s Estate 
with funding prioritised from the increase in the BRP multiplier.

34.0 Deciding on services we provide going forward: statutory versus 
discretionary activity 
34.1 Activity analysis demonstrates that City Fund discretionary expenditure 
is slightly higher than its statutory expenditure, including services delivered 
not only for the Square Mile but also und er the Corporation’s national and 
pan-London responsibilities - see below. While these activities continue to 
contribute significantly to the City’s distinctive role and wider public value, 
they also increase financial exposure as funding becomes more 
constrained. Within the anticipated future funding envelope, it is unlikely to 
be affordable to maintain all existing discretionary services alongside the 
current gold-standard level of statutory provision. Members will therefore 
need to take explicit decisions on the future scope, scale and prioritisation 
of services, including which activities should continue to be delivered 
locally, pan-London or nationally, and at what service level: 
34.1.1 National and pan-London responsibilities City Fund currently 
underwrites several national and pan-London statutory functions 
(Heathrow Animal Reception Centre, London Port Authority, 
London Archives, support to the Central Criminal Court, Walbrook 
Wharf refuse service, New Spitalfields Market and joint 
responsibility with the GLA for providing a grant to the Museum of 
London including capital costs).  In the absence of guaranteed 
government support, continued financing of these responsibilities 

=== PDF PAGE 19 ===
from City Fund is not financially sustainable, particularly given the 
City’s atypical funding model, where services must be delivered 
for a small residential population but an exceptionally large 
daytime workforce and visitor population. 
 
The diversion of City Fund resources to meet national and pan -
London obligations has a direct and mater ial impact on the 
Corporation’s ability to deliver local services at the required scale 
– notably community safety, cleansing, public realm, cultural 
provision, and core corporate capacity. These pressures are 
magnified by the operational reality that loca l services must be 
designed and delivered to support a population far in excess of 
that used within the resident driven funding mechanism. Without 
external funding to reflect this role, continued support for national 
and pan -London functions increasingly d isplaces funding from 
local statutory and priority discretionary services, weakening 
resilience and service quality. 
 
Engagement with MHCLG is ongoing to explore off -formula 
funding solutions for these responsibilities, however this does not 
negate the nee d for Members to make explicit medium -term 
decision on the maximum level of City Fund resource that can be 
committed. Absent clarity and constraint, the City faces a 
structural trade-off in which maintaining national and pan-London 
services ultimately undermines the Corporation’s ability to serve 
its residents, workers, students and visitor as a local authority. 
 
35.0 New approach to savings - budget methodology and governance 
Existing savings processes  and income generation initiatives, primarily 
progressed through officer -led Star Chambers, have largely focused on 
managing in -year cost pressures and mitigating departmental overspends. 
While necessary, this approach has not delivered the scale and pace of cross-
cutting change required to address the MTFP gap at a corporate level. The 
scope for further incremental efficiency savings is now limited and will not be 
sufficient to close future structural deficits.  
 
Addressing the scale of challenge will require a fundamentally dif ferent 
approach to budget setting and governance, with clear corporate direction, 
stronger prioritisation and explicit trade -offs between services. This in turn 
necessitates collective ownership by Members across committees of decisions 
on service scope, s tandards and delivery models, rather than reliance on 
individual departments to manage pressures in isolation. Members discussed 
at your January Finance Committee the implications of the local government 
and police funding settlements, which reinforced the need for a more strategic, 
coordinated approach to savings and financial decision-making going forward.

City Fund Reserves 
49.0 Reserves are a crucial component of financial planning. They serve two primary 
purposes: to mitigate risks, or to invest in the Corporation’s priorities. City Fund 
holds two categories of reserves, usable and unusable: 
i. Usable reserves are defined as those that the Local Authority could utilise 
to fund capital or revenue expenditure. Some of these reserves could  be 
applied generally but others (classified as Earmarked) will have stipulations 
attached on their use. 
ii. Unusable reserves hold unrealised gains or losses for assets not yet 
disposed of and accounting adjustments, which are required by statue. 
These reserves cannot be used to fund capital or revenue expenditure. 
 
50.0 City Fund has a statutory requirement to set a balanced budget each year and 
over the medium-term financial plan after taking account of the use of reserves.  
As a result, usable reserves are  monitored to ensure there are sufficient 
resources to meet this requirement and also to fund the requirements of the 
Capital programme.  The key useable reserves included are; 
i. General Reserves – This is the ‘General Fund Balance’ held at a 
minimum balance of £20m. 
ii. Business Rate Risk Reserve – Held to help smooth the timing 
differences of business rate income hitting the general fund. 
iii. Transitional Reserve  – This is proposed to hold all balances of 
transitional funding received from government.  This will then be applied 
to fund those services provided for the national or pan London benefit, 
or that are driven by the high level of daily commuters.

Robustness of Estimates and Adequacy of Reserves and Contingencies 
83.0 Section 25 of the Local Government Act 2003 requires the Chamberlain  to 
report on the robustness of estimates made for the purpose  of the budget 
calculations and the adequacy of reserves underpinning the budget proposals  
and requires members to have regard to that report in setting the budget. 
 
84.0 Reserves can be classed as either general balances or earmarked reserves 
and represent funds that are not part of the normal recurring budget of the 
council but are distinct ‘pots’ of finite funds. General reserves are held as a 
safeguard against unforeseen events and to ensure the Corporation has 
sufficient funds to meet cash flow requirements. Earmarked reserves are held 
for specific purposes to meet operational commitments, risks or for strategic 
investment. 
 
85.0 In coming to a conclusion on the robustness of estimates, the Chamberlai n 
needs to assess the risk of over or under spending the budget. Having reviewed 
the budget for 2026/27 and the MTFP through 2029/30 , the Chamberlain is  
satisfied that the estimates are sufficiently robust for the purposes of setting 
the budget and that the level and application of General Fund  and 
Earmarked reserves and contingencies is adequate for 2026/27, subject to 
the risk caveats set out below  and the delivery of the agreed measures, 

=== PDF PAGE 37 ===
including the disciplined use of transitional funding and the sav ings and 
transformation programme. Members should note that there is a structural gap 
in core funding, the impact of which will surface in 2029/30. Unless government 
funding is provided for the services that the City Corporation provides f or 
London and the nation, radical service decisions will be required.  
 
86.0 The HRA reserves are extremely precarious both in cash terms and as a 
percentage of rents. The HRA recovery plan sets out a p ath to fiscal 
sustainability, including boosting reserves f rom £0.3m to around £ 2.2m by 
2029/30. 
 
Basis for this opinion 
Robustness of Estimates 
 
87.0 Budget setting process and challenge:  Developing budget estimates for a 
given financial year is an ongoing iterative process within the context of medium 
term and longer-term financial planning. Departments identified pressures and 
deliverable savings; high  pressure areas were challenged through Star 
Chambers. Estimates have been prepared on prudent assumptions, with 
central contingencies carried forward from 2025/26 to manage inflationary and 
other economic risks, which have been evaluated in so far as is po ssible. The 
City Surveyor has carried out an in -depth review of rent incomes . The City 
Corporation has a recent track record of delivering to budget which gives further 
confidence on the robustness of estimates. 
 
88.0 Remaining debt free: the estimates and financial forecast have been prepared 
at this stage on the basis of the Corporation remaining debt free until such time 
as internal borrowing may be needed to bridge the gap for major capital projects 
(the Museum of London relocation an d the Salisbury Square Development 
project). 
 
89.0 Projects and operational estate:  Although the capital programme is 
substantial, the inclusion of 50% optimism bias for major programmes gives 
confidence in the adequacy of budgets. Delivery and affordability risks persist 
given scale and timing, but are well understood and governance arrangements 
are strong , with gateway controls and affordability checks . Additionally, 
provision has been made to support the forward plan of cyclical w orks on 
operational properties through reprioritisation of reserves, provision has been 
included to support the first phase of the Barbican renewal works.  However, 
the full cost of essential works at the Barbican Centre exceeds current 
estimates and there fore requires a fundamental review on how to meet the 
extensive refurbishment needs at the Barbican Centre supported by a 10 -year 
business plan/operating model. I am satisfied that appropriate provision has 
been made across the short term and medium term. Over the longer term, a 
different approach is required to ensure the Corporation can meet the costs of 
its operational estate with recommendations provided in the main report.   
 
90.0 Tax and income levers: The budget assumes Council Tax +4.99% (2% ASC, 
2.99% core) and +1.0p Business Rates Premium (BRP), with 0.4p earmarked 
for FPEP and security and the remainder for daytime population services; each 

=== PDF PAGE 38 ===
0.1p equate to £2.1m . These are reasonable and validated in the working 
papers referenced in the report. 
 
Recognition of key risks 
 
91.0 Uncertainty in core funding : The Final Local Government Settlement  
provides a three -year transitional funding  profile (confirmed for 2026/27, 
indicative thereafter). The MTFP prudently ringfences transitional funding to 
national, pan-London and daytime  population cost pressures and proposes 
transfer of balances to a Transitional Funding Reserve . This approach is 
appropriate but time limited and does not remove the structural gap in core 
funding post 2028/29. Underlying service gap (excluding transitional funding 
and or I&E reserves ) begins c. £13m in 2027/28, rising to £23m by 2029/30, 
requiring savings of that order, alongside the targeted application of both 
income sources and use of Transitional Funding Reserves.  
 
92.0 A larger step change risk from 2029/30  remains if national , pan-London 
responsibilities (c£39m p.a.) are not sustainably grant funded and as retained 
growth and resets in the business rates system reprofile funding. 
 
93.0 Police settlement and national roles:  The 2026/27 police settlement  
provides partial relief (incl. NPG) but is frontloaded; grant confirmation for 
national responsibilities (fraud/economic/cyber) remains uncertain. 
 
Adequacy of Reserves 
 
94.0 Five year position and use of reserves: The plan balances “one year with the 
next”, resulting in a net £ 28.9m surplus  over the MTFP after proposed 
applications (Table 1), supported by managed drawdowns and earmarking 
across working capital, business rates risk, transitional, operational property, 
CWP, and I&E reserves. This provides short term resilience in 2026/27 –
2028/29 but useable reserves reduce materially by 2029/30 , increasing 
exposure thereafter. 
 
95.0 Reserves adequacy and trajectory:  General Fund Balance  maintained at 
£20m for working capital is appropriate as a minimum  given volatility. 
Earmarked reserves stand at £ 258m (as shown in table 1) and  are carefully 
purposed; however, aggregate usable reserves are forecast to decline 
materially over the MTFP, limiting capacity to absorb new shocks, especially 
beyond 2028/29 .  Based on the latest published CIPFA resilience i ndex 
findings, the Corporation’s reserve balances are c30% higher than the top 
London borough. 
 
96.0 HRA fragility:  The HRA remains financially fragile , dependent on 
delivery/occupation of new units and facing compliance pressures. The in-year 
position (2025/26) has required continual action to remain balanced.  Over 
previous years, reserves have been depleted to fund necessary improvement 
works and until additional properties being completed as part of new 
developments, the financial position is extreme ly fragile. Further delays in the 

=== PDF PAGE 39 ===
occupation of the new developments would significantly worsen the outlook and 
a multi -departmental team reviews progress regularly to mitigate the risk. 
Further costs arising from new regulatory health and safety complianc e 
pressures or loss of income in the coming years cannot be absorbed within the 
existing HRA funding envelope. Conseq uently, an Exceptional Financial 
Support (EFS) request of £2.6m for 2026/27 is submitted to MHCLG; failure or 
delay would materially worsen the outlook. 
 
Required management actions and assurances - In giving this opinion, I am 
relying on the following controls through the period: 
 
• Strict discipline of transitional funding:  Use only to address national, pan-
London and daytime  population gaps, with uncommitted balances 
transferred to the Transitional Funding Reserve; no new recurrent 
commitments against these funds. 
• Savings and transformation delivery:  Deliver £13m by 2027/28 . Plan for 
c£18m by 2028/29 and c£23m by 2029/30  on the re-baselined corporate 
approach to prioritisation/governance; report progress and corrective actions 
quarterly. 
• Continuation of workstreams within the transformation programme will 
realign existing resources to new corporate priorities, where this is not possible 
to create headroom to reallocate funds through income generation – noting 
finding sustainable efficiencies will require time, capacity and upfront 
investment which has been requested under one-off measures. 
• BRP governance and earmarking: Maintain the 0.4p allocation (0.3p FPEP; 
0.1p security) and apply remaining BRP to daytime  population services as set 
out; monitor receipts vs assumptions (0.1p = £2.1m) and adjust 
applications/reserves accordingly. 
• Reserve safeguards: Retain General Fund Balance at £20m and deploying 
earmarked reserves only in accordance with their purpose.  
• HRA recovery oversight: Track capital programme and new unit delivery and 
occupancy milestones; escalate mitigations if income or cost assumptions slip. 
Robust spend controls. 
• Major projects control:  Retain gateway controls and affordability checks; 
continue to deploy BRP to qualifying Court security costs. 
• Scenario and stress testing:  Update key assumptions  for: (i ) transitional 
allocations variance, (ii) business rates reset/growth risk, (iii) inflation and (iv) 
capital/cashflow timing; bring back early corrective options  this year and 
pre2027/28.

for 2026/27 are robust and that reserves and contingencies are adequate 
for the forthcoming year, the HRA reserves are extremely low and there is 
key dependency of the success of the HRA recovery plan to avoid

Key risks and uncertainties  
98.0 Fundamental uncertainty regarding central government funding – Fundamental 
uncertainty on core funding from 2029/30.  
   
99.0 Climate Action  – with the current budget envelope expiring at the end of 
2026/27, additional funding will be required to support delivery of the 2040 net 
zero and climate resilience targets between 2027/28 and 2039/40. A 
paper was endorsed by  Policy & Resources Committe e in January 2025 to 
develop the next evolution of the Climate Action Strategy. Following further 
iterations, costed options for the future strategy are to be to Committee in Spring 
2026 with estimates between £6m - 10m annually up to 2033.  
 
100.0 Inflation and interest rates – inflation has reduced from the peaks over 10% but 
has still remained above the government ’s target of 2%.  Interest rates have 
been cut during 2025 but no significant further reductions are expected.  
Despite this, the impact of previous inflation remains significant and future 
projections need to be considered carefully. 
 
101.0 Collection Fund surplus/deficit timing – The Collection Fund is the mechanism 
by which Council Tax and Business Rates income is collected and processed 
through the City Fund accounts.  The timing of when changes in collection rates, 
provisions and appeals can make the amounts flowing through the revenue 
budget fluctuate significantly.  The proposed changes to Business rates make 
forecasting these income streams very difficult on a year-by-year basis.  Work 
is ongoing with external partners to ensure forecasts are as accurate as 
possible and updated where new information comes available. 
 
102.0 ERP implementation –  The Corporation must adopt best practice processes. 
Key benefits are to support a more mobile workforce; automate processes and 
introduce AI capabilities through a modern platform; provide direct access to 
staff and free up strategic capacity; provide a singl e source of the truth with 
enhanced analytics. The first modules of the new system have now gone live 
with more planned during 2026.  If the Corporation fails to adopt to new ways 
of working the consequence will be that the current manually intensive 
processes with inbuilt failure demand will continue and the directly planned 
benefits of £600k pa (which are planned to commence in 2026/27 full finance 
go live) will not be realised in additional to impact the wider organisation 
transformation planned benefits of £500k p.a. 
 
103.0 Ongoing asset maintenance  – In addition to the current Cyclical Works 
Programme, significant future costs are projected in maintaining the 
Corporation’s property portfolio.  A review of the  long-term needs for 
the operational property estat e has  identified c£1.7bn of required investment 

=== PDF PAGE 41 ===
across City Fund, against a net asset position of  c£2.4bn (as at 31 March 
2025). The scale and cost of the operational property 
estate raise fundamental questions about which services the Corporation 
should continue to deliver and which assets are strategically essential, versus 
those that may no longer align with future priorities. This will need to be 
reviewed together with the activity analysis  that is  being prepared. Resource 
Allocation Sub Committee and Policy and Resources Committee will be asked 
to guide the strategic direction of the estate and  establish principles for 
retention, renewal, or disposal, all of which will inevitably depend on defining 
the funding strategy. 
  
104.0 In addition to this extreme uncertainty around ongoing funding, City Fund also 
faces challenges in accommodating on -going pressures, particularly with the 
deficit pressure in 2027/28 being imminent.

Conclusion 
106.0 There is fundamental uncertainty in core funding from 2029/30 onwards which 
the City Corporation needs to address. This will require both political advocacy 
for appropriate funding levels for the sta tutory services that are provided for 
London and nation, and a radical approach to the services that the City 
Corporation provides from its local government fund. The key immediate risk 
remains the fragility of the HRA and the Chamberlain’s s25 report highlights the 
key dependency on the HRA recovery plan and approval of the EFS application 
in compliance with regulations.   
 
107.0 Decisions are required as to the approach to addressing the projected future 
cyclical works and forward plan on our operational properties following the 
resolution of the backlog of maintenance work.   
 
108.0 The scale of the ambition, within City Fund Major Pr ojects in particular 
continues to put significant pressure on resources and work to ensure 
programmes deliver longer term benefits and financial sustainability are key.
